# Sparkles: A Field Guide to Goodness

*Philosophies, religious messages, ideologies, strategies and actions, measured on one scale of good*

---

## How to Read This Book

This book grew out of one conversation and one idea: that goodness is like income or distance travelled. It can be measured, it accumulates, and it never drops below zero. "Bad," by contrast, only appears when someone draws a baseline and points at whoever falls below it.

To compare very different ideas, every chapter uses a single invented unit, the **sparkle**. The scale runs from **0** (the desire to destroy the whole universe) to **500** (perfect moral objectivity). Nothing on it is negative, in keeping with the book's central philosophy.

Each chapter carries three numbers:

- **Told** is how much good a person would likely do after simply being *told* the idea. This is the number the book is sorted by, and it rewards ideas that survive a first hearing without being misunderstood.
- **Believed** is how much good a person would likely do if they *truly understood and believed* it. Nuanced ideas usually rise here; fear-based ideas often fall.
- **Likely true** is a rough probability that the idea is correct, judged by how well it fits evidence and how much it conflicts with other well-supported theories. For philosophical positions it draws partly on the 2020 PhilPapers survey of professional philosophers. Actions and strategies get no truth score (they are things you do, not claims), nor do religious messages (they rest on faith claims evidence cannot settle) or mainstream political ideologies (they mix values with contested empirical debates).

All numbers are estimates. They are meant to start conversations, not end them.

Each chapter follows the same pattern: a short summary, a longer look at the idea **in depth**, the key figure, the key text, when and why the idea arose, other notable voices, related ideas, pros and cons, and anything else worth knowing. Most chapters end with the idea **in conversation**, a short dialogue between Ari, a curious skeptic, and someone who holds the view, and a few things to **try**.

Chapters are ordered by their **told** score, from highest to lowest, so different kinds of ideas sit side by side. Each chapter is labeled with its group: **your principles** and **your former beliefs**, **philosophies**, **strategies**, **religious messages**, **political ideologies**, and **actions**. Where scores tie, your own principles come first.

A few group-specific notes:

- **Religious messages** score specific teachings, not whole religions, because each tradition contains several messages with opposite effects.
- **Political ideologies** are described by their root assumption about the goal of government. Two frameworks help: Thomas Sowell's "constrained" vision (human nature is limited, so work within trade-offs) versus his "unconstrained" vision (human nature is improvable, so pursue solutions), and Jonathan Haidt's finding that left and right both value fairness but define it differently, as equality or as proportionality.
- **Actions** receive the same score for told and believed, and no truth score, since they are deeds rather than claims.

An appendix gathers every score in one table.

## 1. Veganism

*Group: Action · Told: 375 (range 350–400) · Believed: 375 · Likely true: not assessed (an action)*

**In summary.** Avoiding all animal products, in food and often clothing, to reduce animal suffering and environmental harm.

**In depth.**

The core argument is short. Animals that can feel pain have interests, and causing them unnecessary suffering is wrong. For most people in wealthy countries, animal products are not necessary for health, so most of the suffering involved in producing them is unnecessary. Peter Singer framed this as a question of equal consideration: a pig's pain matters as much as a dog's or a child's of similar intensity, and refusing to count it because of species is what he called *speciesism*, a bias like racism or sexism.

The scale is what gives the argument its weight. Tens of billions of land animals are slaughtered for food each year, most raised in industrial systems where chickens are bred to grow so fast their legs struggle to carry them and pigs spend much of their lives in crates or crowded pens. Fish, counted in the hundreds of billions or more, are rarely included in welfare rules at all. Even if you weigh an animal's experience at a small fraction of a human's, the totals are enormous.

Veganism also has an environmental case that stands on its own. Poore and Nemecek's 2018 study, covering about 38,000 farms in 119 countries, found that animal products use most of the world's farmland while providing well under a fifth of its calories, and that shifting toward plant-based diets is one of the largest single ways an individual can cut their food emissions, land use, and water pollution.

A common misreading is that veganism is about personal purity. Most vegan writers describe it as reducing harm "as far as is possible and practicable," the Vegan Society's own wording. Someone who eats mostly plants, or cuts out the most harmful products first (factory-farmed chicken and eggs cause more suffering per calorie than beef, because each animal yields so little food), captures much of the benefit. The book's wide range, 350 to 400, reflects that difference between a strict practice and a thoughtful, sustainable one.

**Key figures.** Donald Watson (1910–2005), who coined "vegan"; Peter Singer.

**Key texts.** Singer's *Animal Liberation* (1975); Tom Regan's *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983).

**Origins and context.** Watson and Elsie Shrigley founded the Vegan Society in England in 1944. Vegetarianism goes back to Pythagoras and to Jain and Hindu traditions. Modern veganism grew with concern over factory farming and climate.

**Other voices.** Jeremy Bentham; Poore and Nemecek's 2018 *Science* study on food's environmental impact.

**Pros.**
- Reduces animal suffering at scale.
- Lower environmental footprint.

**Cons.**
- Requires planning (vitamin B12 supplementation).
- Cost, access, and cultural barriers.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** I like animals, but I don't see why my lunch is a moral question.
>
> **Vegan:** Would you kick a dog for fun?
>
> **Ari:** Of course not.
>
> **Vegan:** Why not?
>
> **Ari:** Because it would hurt, and it would be pointless.
>
> **Vegan:** A pig can hurt at least as much as a dog. Most of the meat we buy comes from pigs and chickens raised in conditions we'd call cruel if we saw them done to a dog. And for most of us, it isn't needed. We'd be healthy without it.
>
> **Ari:** But one person giving up meat doesn't change anything.
>
> **Vegan:** Demand sets supply. Economists estimate that buying less meat does reduce how much is produced, not one-for-one, but substantially. Over a lifetime, one person's choices add up to thousands of animals.
>
> **Ari:** Going fully vegan sounds exhausting.
>
> **Vegan:** Then don't start fully. Swap the things that cause the most suffering first, like cheap chicken and caged eggs. Take B12. Find three plant meals you genuinely love. Most of the good comes from the first big steps, not from perfection.
>
> **Ari:** So it's less a rule and more a direction.
>
> **Vegan:** Exactly. Less harm, as far as you can, starting today.

**Try it.**

- Pick one product that causes a lot of suffering per calorie, such as factory-farmed chicken or caged eggs, and replace it for a month.
- Learn three plant-based meals you'd happily eat every week.
- If you go fully vegan, take a B12 supplement and check your iron, iodine and omega-3 intake.

## 2. Achievable Duties

*Group: Religious message · Told: 340 · Believed: 360 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Concrete, reachable obligations that turn compassion into routine: Islamic zakat (annual giving, typically 2.5% of qualifying wealth), Sikh seva (selfless service) and langar (free communal kitchens), and Jewish tzedakah (charity as justice) and mitzvot (commandments).

**In depth.**

What unites zakat, seva, langar and tzedakah is that they make goodness *specific*. Instead of a vague instruction to "be generous," each tradition says how much, to whom, and how often. Zakat is roughly 2.5% of qualifying wealth held for a year, paid to defined categories of people in need. Tzedakah, from the Hebrew root for justice, treats giving as an obligation owed to the poor rather than an optional kindness, and Jewish law traditionally suggests giving around a tenth of income. Sikh seva asks for regular hands-on service, and langar turns it into a daily institution: anyone, of any background, can sit and eat for free.

Psychologically, achievable duties solve a real problem. Ideals with no stopping point ("give until it hurts") can paralyze people with guilt or lead them to give up. A clear target that can be *met* lets people feel they have done their part, while still leaving room to do more. Maimonides's eight levels of giving show the same practical wisdom: giving anonymously ranks above giving for recognition, and the highest level is helping someone stand on their own, through a loan, a partnership or a job, so they no longer need charity at all.

These duties are also social. Giving happens through mosques, gurdwaras and synagogues, alongside other people, on a calendar. That makes them habits rather than heroic acts, and habits are what sustain behavior over decades. The book scores this message highly for exactly that reason: it reliably turns compassion into food, money and service.

The main risk is the floor becoming a ceiling. A fixed minimum can make some people stop exactly there, and giving that stays within one's own community, while valuable, can leave distant and greater needs unmet.

**Key figures.** The Prophet Muhammad (c. 570–632); Guru Nanak (1469–1539); Moses Maimonides (1138–1204).

**Key texts.** The Quran; the Guru Granth Sahib; Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah*, which ranks eight levels of giving, the highest being helping someone become self-sufficient.

**Origins and context.** Zakat became one of Islam's five pillars in seventh-century Arabia, institutionalizing care for the poor. Guru Nanak founded Sikhism in Punjab amid caste division; langar, where everyone sits on the floor and eats as equals, deliberately challenged caste. The Golden Temple's langar serves tens of thousands daily. Jewish tzedakah developed over millennia of diaspora communities supporting their own.

**Other voices.** Guru Amar Das, who made langar central; the Talmud's sages.

**Related ideas.** Tithing; effective altruism's giving pledges; mutual aid.

**Pros.**
- Clear targets avoid the guilt of impossible ideals.
- Community support makes them sustainable.
- Directs real resources to people in need.

**Cons.**
- Can become box-ticking without inner change.
- Giving may stay within one's own community.
- Fixed minimums can make some stop at the minimum.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Why would a fixed number like 2.5% be better than just being generous?
>
> **Neighbour:** Because "be generous" never tells you when you've done it. People either feel guilty forever or quietly give nothing.
>
> **Ari:** And a number fixes that?
>
> **Neighbour:** It gives you something you can actually finish. You pay your zakat, you serve at the langar on Sunday, you set aside your tzedakah. Done, and done again next year.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that make it box-ticking?
>
> **Neighbour:** It can. That's why the traditions also care about how you give. Maimonides ranked giving anonymously above giving for praise, and helping someone become self-sufficient highest of all.
>
> **Ari:** What if I can afford a lot more than the minimum?
>
> **Neighbour:** Then the minimum is your floor, not your limit. The point is that nobody has an excuse to give nothing.

**Try it.**

- Choose a giving rule you can keep, such as a fixed percentage of income, and set it up as an automatic transfer.
- Volunteer somewhere hands-on on a regular schedule, even once a month.
- Next time you give, ask whether there's a way to help someone become self-sufficient rather than only relieving a need.

## 3. The Combined System

*Group: Your principle · Told: 330 · Believed: 360 · Likely true: 55%*

**In summary.** Six principles working together. Behavior has causes, so understand and mitigate them rather than simply "trying harder" (compassionate determinism with illusionism). Unmet expectations are the root of unnecessary suffering (relative deprivation theory). Goodness has a floor at zero but no ceiling, so appreciate life while always improving it (moving towards limitless good). "Bad" is a signal societies use to teach what they value, not a verdict on a person (bad as a learning signal). Every philosophy has value, measured by why you follow it and how it helps (diversity of philosophy). Truth comes in degrees, valued by consistency, utility and simplicity (truth valued by consistency, utility, simplicity).

**In depth.**

The system starts from a reframe of what goodness is. Most moral talk sorts people and acts into "good" and "bad," as if there were a line with everyone on one side or the other. This system replaces the line with a scale that starts at zero and has no top. Goodness behaves like income or distance travelled: it accumulates, it can always grow, and it never goes negative. "Bad" is what we call the region below some baseline that a person or society has drawn, and the choice of baseline is what varies.

Six principles hold the picture up, and each answers a question the others raise. *Why do people fall short?* Because behaviour has causes (compassionate determinism with illusionism), so the useful response is to understand and change those causes, while still using the felt sense of choice to pick the better option. *Why do people suffer so much over it?* Because of unmet expectations (relative deprivation), so releasing demands removes a great deal of needless pain. *Then why keep trying?* Because good has no ceiling (moving towards limitless good), so there is always somewhere further to go. *What do we do with blame?* Treat "bad" as a signal a community uses to teach what it values (bad as a learning signal): hear it, learn from it, and let it go. *What about other people's beliefs?* Every philosophy can carry some value, judged by why it's held and how it helps (diversity of philosophy). *And how do we judge ideas at all?* Truth comes in degrees, weighed by consistency, utility and simplicity.

The mud-and-pavement image ties it together. Two people walking the same distance may have done very different amounts of work, depending on the ground under their feet: money, health, upbringing, safety, luck. So the system never ranks people against each other. It asks only one thing of each person: from where you stand, on the ground you're on, keep walking.

The principles also guard each other. Releasing expectations could drift into complacency, but limitless good keeps pushing. Constant improvement could turn into guilt, but understanding causes keeps it gentle. And because no one is written off, accountability never needs shame.

**The core image.** Goodness is distance travelled. Some people walk on pavement; others walk through mud. Someone who moves a little through mud may be working harder than someone who strolls far on a smooth road. So people are not compared to each other, and no one is written off. Everyone is accountable, always, for the most good they can do from where they stand.

**Where it came from.** The system was built from lived experience across many countries, then refined in conversation. Its conclusion, that goodness is objective and limitless while badness depends on a chosen baseline, came first. The author later recognized that conclusion as the *litmus reading*, and the six principles as the underlying chemistry.

**Closest relatives.** Augustine and Aquinas on evil as privation; Alastair Norcross's *Morality by Degrees* (2020), which treats right and wrong as arbitrary cutoffs on a scale of better and worse; Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel on moral luck; Robert Sapolsky on determinism; the Buddha on craving; William James on meliorism and pragmatism.

**Pros.**
- Combines compassion (for others and oneself) with relentless forward motion.
- The principles cover each other's weaknesses: releasing expectations could become complacency, but limitless good keeps pushing; constant improvement could breed guilt, but understanding motivators keeps it gentle.
- Holds everyone accountable without shaming anyone.

**Cons.**
- Its headline ("nothing is bad") is easily misheard as permission, which is why the system is best taught through its principles.
- Two pieces are minority philosophical positions: rejecting free will entirely, and treating badness as mere absence.
- Can feel dismissive to someone describing harm done to them unless framed carefully.

**Worth knowing.** A one-line version: *"Goodness is the distance you travel, not where you end up. So don't judge the traveler, measure the road, and keep walking."* Another: *"Bad is a signal, not a verdict."*

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** You say nothing is bad. So anything goes?
>
> **Author:** No. Nothing goes below zero. Some things are far closer to zero than others, and everyone can always move further up.
>
> **Ari:** What's the point of saying it that way?
>
> **Author:** It changes how we treat people. If someone is "bad," we write them off. If they're low on the scale, the question becomes how to help them move.
>
> **Ari:** Where does the mud come in?
>
> **Author:** Some people walk on pavement, some through mud. Someone who moves a little through mud might be working harder than someone who strolls far on a smooth road. So I don't compare travellers.
>
> **Ari:** Then who's accountable?
>
> **Author:** Everyone, always, for the most good they can do from where they stand. That never stops. It just doesn't come with shame.
>
> **Ari:** Give me the one-line version.
>
> **Author:** Goodness is the distance you travel, not where you end up. So don't judge the traveller, measure the road, and keep walking.

**Try it.**

- Think of someone you've judged harshly and ask what ground they've been walking on.
- Write down one step further you could go today, from exactly where you are.

## 4. Graded Vows

*Group: Religious message · Told: 330 · Believed: 350 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** A demanding ideal of nonviolence (*ahimsa*) and nonattachment, with two levels of commitment: strict great vows for monks and nuns, and lighter "small vows" for laypeople.

**In depth.**

Jainism takes nonviolence (*ahimsa*) further than almost any other tradition. Its central insight is that all living beings, down to insects and even plants and microorganisms, have souls (*jiva*) and can suffer, so every action carries some harm. Since harm can never be reduced to zero while living, the question becomes how far each person can reasonably go.

The answer is two levels of vow. Monks and nuns take the five *mahavratas*, the great vows, in their strictest form: no harm to any being, absolute truthfulness, taking nothing not freely given, complete celibacy, and owning almost nothing. Some ascetics sweep the ground before them to avoid stepping on insects and wear cloth over their mouths. Laypeople take the *anuvratas*, the small vows: the same five principles adjusted for ordinary life, such as avoiding intentional violence, being honest in business, staying faithful in marriage, and limiting possessions. Many Jains are strict vegetarians who also avoid root vegetables, since uprooting a plant kills it and the organisms in the soil.

The structure is what earns this message its high score. It holds up an extraordinary ideal while making room for people where they are: everyone is walking the same road, just at different distances. That mirrors the book's own image of goodness as distance travelled.

Jainism also pairs nonviolence of action with nonviolence of thought, through *anekantavada*, the many-sidedness of truth. The classic illustration is the blind men and the elephant: each touches one part and describes a different animal, and each is partly right. Humility about one's own view is treated as part of not doing harm.

**Key figure.** Mahavira (traditionally 599–527 BCE), the 24th Tirthankara of Jainism.

**Key text.** The *Tattvartha Sutra* by Umaswati (early centuries CE), accepted by all Jain sects.

**Origins and context.** Mahavira taught in the Ganges plain alongside the Buddha, in an era of spiritual reform challenging Vedic animal sacrifice. Jainism's five vows (nonviolence, truthfulness, not stealing, chastity, nonpossession) are taken fully by ascetics and in moderated form by householders.

**Other voices.** Mohandas Gandhi, deeply influenced by the Jain teacher Shrimad Rajchandra.

**Related ideas.** Vegetarianism and veganism; *anekantavada* (many-sidedness of truth); Buddhist precepts.

**Pros.**
- A high ideal with a realistic entry point.
- Profound respect for all life.
- Jain communities are known for philanthropy and nonviolence.

**Cons.**
- Strict versions are extremely demanding.
- Two-tier systems can imply laypeople are lesser.
- Small community; limited global reach.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Not even stepping on insects? That seems impossible to live by.
>
> **Jain:** For most of us it is, which is why there are two levels. Monks and nuns take the great vows. Householders like me take the small ones.
>
> **Ari:** So the rules are softer for ordinary people?
>
> **Jain:** The direction is the same, only the distance differs. I don't intentionally harm anything, I don't eat meat, I try to be honest in business, and I limit what I own.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that make laypeople second-class?
>
> **Jain:** Some critics say so. I see it as a path. You start where you can, and you can always take on more.
>
> **Ari:** What about disagreements? Nonviolence can't settle every argument.
>
> **Jain:** That's what *anekantavada* is for. The truth has many sides, so I try to assume the other person sees a part of it I can't. Even arguing can be done without violence.

**Try it.**

- Pick one "small vow" you could keep for a week, such as no intentional harm to any creature, or one day without buying anything.
- In your next disagreement, name one thing the other person sees that you might be missing.

## 5. Effective Altruism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 320 · Believed: 340 · Likely true: 70%*

**In summary.** Use evidence and reason to figure out how to do the most good with your time and money, then act on it. Some charities are hundreds of times more effective than others, so choosing well matters as much as giving.

**In depth.**

Effective altruism starts from two observations. First, most people want to do good but rarely check whether their giving works. Second, the difference between options is enormous. GiveWell's research has long estimated that the most cost-effective global health charities, such as those distributing malaria nets, save a life for roughly a few thousand dollars, while many well-meaning programs achieve far less for the same money or cannot show any effect at all.

So the movement asks three questions of any cause. How *big* is the problem? How *tractable* is it: can extra effort actually make progress? And how *neglected* is it: is there already plenty of money, or would yours make a real difference? These criteria led many effective altruists to three main areas: global health and poverty, animal welfare (especially factory farming), and risks that could threaten humanity's long-term future, such as pandemics and unsafe artificial intelligence.

Effective altruism also applies to careers. The organization 80,000 Hours, named after the rough number of hours in a working life, encourages people to choose work that does the most good, whether directly or by "earning to give" and donating a large share of a high income. Many members take the Giving What We Can pledge to give at least 10% of their income.

The FTX collapse in 2022 became a test of the movement's ideas. Critics argued that a philosophy of maximizing impact had made it easy to rationalize risky or dishonest behavior for a greater good. Many effective altruists responded that honesty and respect for rules were always meant to constrain the maximizing, and the episode pushed the community toward more humility, more diverse funding and clearer ethical limits. The core idea, that we should care about the results of our kindness, survives the scandal.

**Key figures.** Peter Singer (born 1946), who laid the groundwork; William MacAskill (born 1987) and Toby Ord (born 1979), who founded the movement.

**Key texts.** Singer's essay "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) and *The Life You Can Save* (2009); MacAskill's *Doing Good Better* (2015); Ord's *The Precipice* (2020).

**Origins and context.** Singer wrote his 1972 essay during the Bangladesh famine, arguing that if you would ruin your shoes to save a child drowning in front of you, distance shouldn't excuse you from saving children far away. The movement formed around 2009–2011 at Oxford, with Ord's Giving What We Can pledge and charity evaluators like GiveWell, during a decade of data-driven optimism.

**Other voices.** Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld (GiveWell); Larissa MacFarquhar's *Strangers Drowning* (2015), profiling extreme altruists.

**Related ideas.** Utilitarianism; randomized controlled trials in development economics (Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, Nobel 2019); longtermism.

**Pros.**
- Has directed large sums to proven interventions like malaria nets and deworming.
- Makes compassion concrete and measurable.
- Encourages humility about one's assumptions.

**Cons.**
- The 2022 collapse of FTX, whose founder Sam Bankman-Fried was a prominent backer, raised fears that ends-justify-means thinking can rationalize wrongdoing.
- Critics argue it undervalues local ties, systemic change, and hard-to-measure goods.
- Its demandingness can produce guilt.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** I already give to charity. Isn't that enough?
>
> **Effective altruist:** Do you know what your donation achieves?
>
> **Ari:** I assume it helps.
>
> **Effective altruist:** Some charities are a hundred times more effective than others. If you could do a hundred times more good with the same money, wouldn't you want to?
>
> **Ari:** Put like that, yes. But how would I know which ones?
>
> **Effective altruist:** Evaluators like GiveWell do that research. They look for programs with strong evidence, like malaria nets, where each extra dollar does a lot.
>
> **Ari:** It sounds cold, turning kindness into spreadsheets.
>
> **Effective altruist:** The spreadsheet is how you take the people you'll never meet seriously. The feeling stays; it just gets directions.
>
> **Ari:** And the FTX scandal?
>
> **Effective altruist:** A hard lesson. Doing the most good never justifies lying or cutting corners. Most of us now say that more loudly.

**Try it.**

- Look up one charity you already support and find out what it achieves per dollar.
- Try giving a fixed share of your income, even 1%, to a highly rated charity for a year.
- Before your next big decision about work or time, ask how much good each option does, not just how good it feels.

## 6. Repentance as a Path Back

*Group: Religious message · Told: 320 · Believed: 340 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Failure is not final. Acknowledge wrongdoing, feel remorse, make amends, and commit to change, and you can return to good standing. Jewish *teshuvah* ("return"), Islamic *tawbah*, and Christian confession all share this.

**In depth.**

Teshuvah literally means "return," and the word captures the idea: wrongdoing is a step away, and you can always step back. Maimonides described four parts of complete repentance. You stop the behavior, you regret it sincerely, you confess it in words (to God, and to the person harmed if you wronged someone), and you resolve not to repeat it. For wrongs against other people, Jewish tradition adds a firm condition: God does not forgive what you did to another person until you have tried to make it right with them, including repaying any loss. On Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, people are expected to seek out those they have hurt *before* the fast.

Islam teaches *tawbah* in similar steps: leaving the sin, regretting it, intending never to return to it, and restoring the rights of anyone wronged. The Quran repeatedly stresses that God's mercy is wider than any sin, so despair is itself a mistake. Christian confession, and the practice of penance, likewise pairs honest acknowledgment with a fresh start.

What makes this message psychologically powerful is that it separates the act from the person. Research by June Tangney and others distinguishes guilt ("I did a bad thing"), which tends to motivate apology and repair, from shame ("I am bad"), which tends to produce hiding, defensiveness and anger. Repentance traditions build a structured path from guilt to repair, and they treat the person who returns as fully restored. That is why the idea feeds directly into restorative justice and into Alcoholics Anonymous's steps of making a moral inventory and making amends.

The weakness is the opposite risk: repentance used as a reset button by someone who keeps causing harm. Maimonides's test guards against that. True return is proven only when you face the same situation again and choose differently.

**Key figure.** Maimonides, whose "Laws of Repentance" codified teshuvah.

**Key texts.** Maimonides's *Hilchot Teshuvah* in the *Mishneh Torah* (12th century); the Quran, which names God *al-Tawwab*, the One who accepts repentance.

**Origins and context.** Teshuvah is rooted in the Hebrew prophets and centered on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement. Maimonides defined complete repentance as facing the same temptation again and choosing differently. The idea later shaped Alcoholics Anonymous (1935), whose steps include making amends.

**Other voices.** Augustine's *Confessions*; Howard Zehr, *Changing Lenses* (1990), founding text of restorative justice.

**Related ideas.** Restorative justice; twelve-step programs; the author's "bad as a learning signal."

**Pros.**
- Makes failure a step, not an identity.
- Requires repairing harm, not just feeling sorry.
- Fits guilt-not-shame research.

**Cons.**
- Can be abused as a reset button for repeated harm.
- Victims may feel pressured to accept apologies.
- Ritual forms can become hollow.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** I really hurt a friend last year. I still feel awful.
>
> **Rabbi:** Feeling awful is the start, not the end. Have you told them you're sorry?
>
> **Ari:** I'm afraid it'll make things worse.
>
> **Rabbi:** In our tradition, you can't skip that step. Saying sorry to God doesn't cover what you owe a person. You go to them, you name what you did, and you offer to repair what you can.
>
> **Ari:** And if they won't forgive me?
>
> **Rabbi:** Try sincerely, more than once. If they still refuse, the burden is no longer yours.
>
> **Ari:** How do I know I've really changed?
>
> **Rabbi:** Maimonides answered that. When the same situation comes again and you choose differently, your return is complete.
>
> **Ari:** So I'm not a bad person forever.
>
> **Rabbi:** You did a bad thing. You are a person who can return. That's the whole point.

**Try it.**

- Write down one harm you did that still bothers you, and what repairing it would look like.
- Apologize to one person this week, naming specifically what you did, without excuses.
- Notice the difference between "I did a bad thing" and "I am bad," and practise saying the first.

## 7. Love Across Difference

*Group: Religious message · Told: 320 · Believed: 350 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Love not only those like you, but strangers, outsiders, and even enemies. Your "neighbor" is anyone in need.

**In depth.**

The parable of the Good Samaritan is famous partly because of its twist. A lawyer asks Jesus which neighbours he is required to love, hoping for a limit. Jesus answers with a story where two respected religious figures pass by a wounded man, and the one who stops to help is a Samaritan, a member of a group many of his listeners regarded as heretics and enemies. Then he turns the question around: not "who counts as my neighbour?" but "who *acted* as a neighbour?" Being a neighbour is something you do, not a category you check.

"Love your enemies" goes further still. Love here does not mean liking, approving or feeling warmth. Martin Luther King Jr. drew on the Greek word *agape* to describe it: an active goodwill that seeks the other person's good even when they oppose you. For King this was practical as well as spiritual. Hate, he argued, multiplies hate, and only love can turn an enemy into a friend. Nonviolent movements built on this principle aimed to win over opponents, not destroy them.

Similar teachings appear across traditions. Mozi, in ancient China, argued for *jian ai*, impartial care, criticizing Confucian ethics for favouring family over strangers. Buddhist *metta* meditation extends loving-kindness step by step: to yourself, a loved one, a neutral person, a difficult person, and finally all beings.

The Darley and Batson study is a sobering footnote. Seminary students on their way to give a talk, some of them about this very parable, were far less likely to stop for a person slumped in a doorway when told they were running late. The lesson isn't that the ideal is hollow. It's that good intentions need room to act: slack in the schedule, attention to spare, and environments that make helping the easy thing to do.

**Key figure.** Jesus of Nazareth.

**Key texts.** The parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37); "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:43–48).

**Origins and context.** Asked "who is my neighbor?", Jesus told of a man beaten by robbers and ignored by a priest and a Levite, then rescued by a Samaritan, a member of a group his listeners despised. In first-century Judea, Jews and Samaritans had centuries of hostility. Making the outsider the hero overturned in-group loyalty.

**Other voices.** Martin Luther King Jr.'s sermon "Loving Your Enemies" in *Strength to Love* (1963); Mozi's ancient Chinese doctrine of impartial care; the Buddhist practice of extending loving-kindness to enemies.

**Related ideas.** Universal human rights; humanitarianism (the Red Cross, founded 1863).

**Pros.**
- Directly counters in-group bias.
- Offers a concrete act: help the person in front of you.
- Inspired major humanitarian movements.

**Cons.**
- Loving enemies can feel impossible, risking guilt.
- A famous 1973 study by Darley and Batson found seminary students in a hurry often passed a person in need, even on the way to speak about this very parable, showing how circumstances override ideals.

**Worth knowing.** That study supports the author's motivations principle: change the circumstances, not just the intentions.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Love my enemies? I can't even like some of my coworkers.
>
> **Pastor:** You don't have to like them. The love Jesus meant is a decision to want their good.
>
> **Ari:** Even if they've wronged me?
>
> **Pastor:** Especially then. That's when it changes things. Hate answered with hate just grows.
>
> **Ari:** And who counts as my neighbour? Everyone on Earth?
>
> **Pastor:** In the parable, Jesus doesn't answer that. He asks who *acted* like a neighbour. It was the outsider, the one nobody expected.
>
> **Ari:** I'd like to think I'd stop for someone hurt on the road.
>
> **Pastor:** So did the seminary students in that famous study, until they were told they were late. Most of us fail because we're rushed, not because we're cruel.
>
> **Ari:** So part of loving people is leaving room in my day to notice them.
>
> **Pastor:** That might be the most practical sermon there is.

**Try it.**

- Do one concrete kind act for someone you find difficult, without telling anyone.
- Try loving-kindness meditation: wish wellbeing to yourself, a friend, a stranger, then someone difficult.
- Leave ten minutes of slack in your schedule today, and use it if someone needs help.

## 8. Compassionate Determinism with Illusionism

*Group: Your principle · Told: 310 · Believed: 340 · Likely true: 35%*

**In summary.** Free will does not exist: every action is the product of genes, upbringing, environment, and circumstance. Understanding those causes makes forgiveness of others and oneself natural. But the *illusion* of free will is useful, so we should deliberately use it to choose the better of our options. Accountability survives in a forward-looking form: holding someone accountable is itself one more cause that shapes what they do next.

**In depth.**

This principle combines two positions that are usually thought to clash. The first is hard determinism: everything a person does comes from causes they didn't choose, from genes and upbringing to the circumstances of the moment. Looked at this way, nobody deserves blame in the deep sense, and understanding the causes of someone's behaviour makes forgiveness natural, including forgiveness of yourself.

The second is illusionism, Saul Smilansky's idea that the *feeling* of free will, though not literally accurate, is worth keeping. In daily life we can't experience ourselves as a sequence of causes, and trying to tends to sap motivation. So the principle recommends using the sense of choice deliberately: when facing options, act as though you're choosing, and choose the better one. The choosing is itself one of the causes.

Accountability survives in a forward-looking form. Holding someone accountable isn't about making them pay for what they "freely" did; it's adding a new cause, feedback, consequences or support, that shapes what they'll do next. The same applies to yourself: instead of berating yourself for a failure, ask what caused it and change those causes. This is where the practical advice comes from: change the environment, not just the intention, because people fall to the level of their systems.

The principle sits at a "likely true" of 35% because most philosophers favour compatibilism, the view that meaningful free will is compatible with determinism, and because deliberately keeping an illusion sits uneasily with valuing truth. The principle's answer is pragmatic: if a belief is useful, consistent with what we observe about behaviour, and simple to live by, it earns its place.

**Key figures.** Robert Sapolsky (born 1957), a Stanford neuroscientist and primatologist, for the compassionate determinism; Saul Smilansky (born 1961), an Israeli philosopher, for illusionism.

**Key texts.** Sapolsky's *Behave* (2017) and *Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will* (2023); Smilansky's *Free Will and Illusion* (2000).

**Origins and context.** Determinism is ancient: the Stoics believed in fate, and Spinoza argued in his *Ethics* (1677) that people think they are free only because they are unaware of the causes of their actions. Sapolsky's version arrived amid growing neuroscience research and debates over criminal justice reform, mass incarceration, and addiction treatment. Smilansky wrote during a period when philosophers were asking what would happen to society if belief in free will collapsed.

**Other voices.** Baruch Spinoza; Arthur Schopenhauer, who said a person can do what they will but cannot will what they will; Sam Harris (*Free Will*, 2012); Derk Pereboom, who argues for "free will skepticism" with a quarantine model of justice.

**Related ideas.** Compatibilism (Daniel Dennett), which keeps responsibility within determinism; the public-health approach to violence; James Clear's line that people fall to the level of their systems.

**Pros.**
- Replaces blame with understanding, which tends to support rehabilitation over revenge.
- Points toward systemic fixes, which often work better than willpower.
- The illusionist twist keeps people feeling capable rather than powerless.

**Cons.**
- Only about 11% of philosophers in the 2020 PhilPapers survey rejected free will; about 59% favored compatibilism.
- Some studies suggested weakening free-will belief increases cheating, though later replications were mixed.
- Deliberately maintaining an illusion sits uneasily with valuing truth.

**Worth knowing.** Sapolsky openly admits he cannot fully live as though free will is absent, which is exactly the gap illusionism fills.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If free will doesn't exist, why try at all?
>
> **Author:** Because trying is one of the causes. Your effort is part of what shapes what happens next.
>
> **Ari:** But you just said the effort isn't really free.
>
> **Author:** True. But the feeling of choosing is useful, so I use it on purpose. When I face two options, I act as if I'm choosing, and I pick the better one.
>
> **Ari:** Isn't that lying to yourself?
>
> **Author:** It's more like using a map that's simpler than the territory. Good enough to navigate by.
>
> **Ari:** And when someone hurts you?
>
> **Author:** I look at what made them do it. It's very hard to hate someone once you see their causes. I still hold them accountable, because that's a cause too, for what they do next.
>
> **Ari:** And your own mistakes?
>
> **Author:** Same thing. Less blame, more redesign. Change what caused it.

**Try it.**

- After your next mistake, list three causes of it and change one, instead of resolving to "try harder."
- When facing a choice today, pause and consciously pick the better option, treating the choosing itself as the lever.

## 9. Relative Deprivation Theory

*Group: Your principle · Told: 310 · Believed: 330 · Likely true: 65%*

**In summary.** Suffering comes less from what we lack than from the gap between what we have and what we expected or see others having. A person can be materially comfortable and still feel deprived, or poor and content. Applied personally: expect less, demand nothing, appreciate what is here, and much unnecessary suffering disappears.

**In depth.**

Relative deprivation explains a puzzle: why people with plenty can feel deprived, and people with little can feel content. The answer is that satisfaction depends less on what we have than on the gap between what we have and what we expected, or what we see others having. Stouffer's soldiers made the point vividly: military police, rarely promoted, were more satisfied with their chances than Air Corps soldiers, who were promoted often, because the airmen compared themselves with peers who had moved up.

Applied personally, the principle becomes a practice: expect less, demand nothing, appreciate what is here. Much suffering comes not from the event itself but from "it should have been otherwise." A delayed train, a friend who forgets your birthday, a career that doesn't follow the plan: each hurts partly because of the event and largely because of the expectation it broke.

The important refinement is the difference between *standards* and *demands*. Releasing demands doesn't mean accepting mistreatment. You can still hold clear standards, such as what you'll accept from a partner or employer, and act when they aren't met, while not insisting that the world owes you a particular outcome. Albert Ellis's distinction between preferences ("I'd like this") and demands ("this must happen") captures it: preferences lead to disappointment when unmet, demands to anguish.

This principle covers mainly the social side of suffering: comparing ourselves with others. Its inner twin, self-discrepancy theory, covers the gap between who we are and who we think we *should* be, which produces shame. The book's other principles help there: understanding causes and treating "bad" as a signal soften that inner gap too.

**Key figures.** Samuel Stouffer, who identified the effect in World War II soldiers; W. G. Runciman, who developed it into a social theory.

**Key texts.** Stouffer and colleagues' *The American Soldier* (1949); Runciman's *Relative Deprivation and Social Justice* (1966); Ted Gurr's *Why Men Rebel* (1970).

**Origins and context.** Stouffer's team surveyed hundreds of thousands of US soldiers and found a puzzle: military police, who were rarely promoted, were more satisfied with promotion chances than Air Corps soldiers, who were promoted often. The explanation was comparison: in a unit where everyone is promoted, being passed over stings. Postwar sociologists then used the idea to explain why revolutions often erupt when conditions are *improving*, as expectations outrun reality.

**Other voices.** The Buddha's second noble truth, that suffering arises from craving; Epictetus, who held that we are disturbed by our judgments about events rather than events themselves; Albert Ellis, whose Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (founded 1955) targets the "musts" that turn preferences into demands; E. Tory Higgins's self-discrepancy theory (1987), which links distress to gaps between the actual and the "ought" self.

**Related ideas.** The hedonic treadmill (Brickman and Campbell, 1971); adaptation-level theory (Harry Helson); reward prediction error in neuroscience; Robb Rutledge's 2014 finding that momentary happiness tracks whether outcomes beat expectations.

**Pros.**
- Explains why people in wealthy countries can feel the world is worse than ever.
- Offers a practical lever: adjust expectations, not just circumstances.
- Fosters gratitude.

**Cons.**
- "Expect nothing" can backfire with people: someone who expects nothing of a partner may tolerate mistreatment. Holding *standards* while releasing *demands* is the safer version.
- Goal-setting research (Locke and Latham) shows high expectations can improve performance.
- Rutledge also found anticipation itself lifts mood, so expectation is not purely a cost.

**Worth knowing.** The theory mainly covers the social side of suffering; self-discrepancy theory covers the inner side, the shame of falling short of who you think you should be.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Expect nothing? Doesn't that mean settling for less?
>
> **Author:** It means not demanding. I can still hold standards and walk away from what doesn't meet them.
>
> **Ari:** What's the difference?
>
> **Author:** A demand says the world must give me this. When it doesn't, I suffer twice: once from the loss, once from the outrage. A standard just tells me what I'll accept.
>
> **Ari:** Why do people feel so deprived when they have so much?
>
> **Author:** Because we measure against expectations and neighbours, not against what we actually have. The soldiers who got promoted most were the least satisfied.
>
> **Ari:** So the trick is lowering expectations?
>
> **Author:** Loosening them. Then good things feel like gifts instead of what you were owed, and bad things hurt once instead of twice.

**Try it.**

- Notice one "should" today ("they should have…") and rewrite it as a preference ("I'd have liked…").
- List three things you have now that you once hoped for.

## 10. Moving Towards Limitless Good

*Group: Your principle · Told: 310 · Believed: 340 · Likely true: 50%*

**In summary.** Badness is not a substance but an absence of good, so nothing falls below zero. And there is no ceiling: everything can always be improved. The world is already good and worth appreciating, and it will never be perfect, so the task is endless and joyful. No matter how good a person is, they can and should be better.

**In depth.**

This principle has two halves that only work together: a floor and an open sky. The floor comes from privation theory: badness isn't a substance with its own existence, but an absence or lack of good, the way darkness is the absence of light and a hole is the absence of material. If that's right, nothing falls below zero; the lowest possible point is simply no good at all. The world, then, is already good wherever good exists, and worth appreciating as it is.

The open sky comes from meliorism, William James's word for the belief that the world can be made better through effort, steering between optimism (it's already as good as it gets) and pessimism (it can't improve). If goodness is something that accumulates, there's no ceiling: any situation, any person, any act could always be better. Perfection is never reached, which isn't a source of despair but of permanent possibility.

Together they produce a particular emotional stance: gratitude and ambition at once. Because the world is good, appreciate it; because it can always be better, improve it. Neither half works alone. Gratitude without ambition becomes complacency; ambition without gratitude becomes exhausting, a sense that nothing is ever enough. The author arrived at the second half through the first: once badness is understood as absence, the only direction left is up, and nothing caps how far.

This connects to James Carse's distinction between finite games, played to win, and infinite games, played to keep playing. Being good, on this view, is an infinite game: there's no final score, only the next move. Kaizen, the Japanese practice of continuous small improvement, is its everyday form.

**Key figures.** Augustine of Hippo (354–430) for the floor; William James (1842–1910) for the forward motion.

**Key texts.** Augustine's *Confessions* (c. 397–400) and *Enchiridion* (c. 420); James's *Pragmatism* (1907), which championed "meliorism."

**Origins and context.** Augustine developed privation theory partly in reaction to Manichaeism, a religion he once followed that taught good and evil were two equal cosmic forces. Rejecting that dualism, he argued evil has no existence of its own. James wrote at the turn of the twentieth century, an era of rapid industrial and scientific progress, positioning meliorism between naive optimism and despairing pessimism.

**Other voices.** Plotinus (c. 204–270), who saw evil as distance from the source of all goodness; Thomas Aquinas, who held that God is infinite goodness in which creatures participate more or less fully; David Deutsch, whose *The Beginning of Infinity* (2011) argues problems are inevitable but soluble; James Carse, whose *Finite and Infinite Games* (1986) distinguishes games played to win from games played to keep playing.

**Related ideas.** Kaizen, the Japanese practice of continuous improvement; growth mindset (Carol Dweck); the bodhisattva vow to save numberless beings.

**Pros.**
- Pairs gratitude with ambition, avoiding both complacency and despair.
- Removes the idea that anyone can "finish" being good.
- Gives the author's income and distance analogies a coherent foundation.

**Cons.**
- Privation theory struggles with experiences like agony, which seem to have a positive presence rather than being a mere lack.
- "Always be better" can become exhausting without the gratitude half.
- Some critics argue an endless task offers no rest point.

**Worth knowing.** The author arrived at limitless good by way of privation theory: once badness is an absence, the only direction left is up, and nothing caps how far.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If you can always be better, won't you never feel good enough?
>
> **Author:** That's why gratitude comes first. The world is already good, and so are you, as far as you've come. Improvement is something extra, not a debt.
>
> **Ari:** And badness is just an absence?
>
> **Author:** Like darkness is just no light. Nothing goes below zero. The lowest point is simply no good, and from there everything is up.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't pain feel like more than an absence?
>
> **Author:** That's the hardest objection, and I take it seriously. But the framework still helps: it tells me where to put effort, toward adding good.
>
> **Ari:** So being good never ends.
>
> **Author:** It's an infinite game. No final score, just the next move, and that's the joyful part.

**Try it.**

- Each evening, write one thing that was good today and one small way tomorrow could be a little better.

## 11. Compassionate Determinism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 310 · Believed: 330 · Likely true: 30%*

**In summary.** Every behavior results from biology and environment, from neurons firing a second before an act back to genes and upbringing. Since no one chooses those causes, no one truly deserves blame or praise. The right response is understanding, compassion, and systems that prevent harm.

**In depth.**

Sapolsky's argument works by zooming out, step by step. Why did someone do what they did? Look a second earlier: neurons fired in the brain. Minutes earlier: something in the environment triggered them. Hours earlier: hormones like cortisol or testosterone shaped how sensitive those neurons were. Months and years earlier: experiences rewired the brain. Earlier still: childhood stress, the mother's health during pregnancy, the genes the person inherited, and the culture their ancestors built. At no point in this chain, he argues, is there room for a "self" that steps outside causation and chooses freely. In *Determined* he argues that we are, in effect, the sum of biological and environmental luck we had no control over.

From this he draws a moral conclusion rather than a bleak one. If nobody authored their own brain, nobody *deserves* suffering as punishment or praise as reward in the deep sense. We don't blame a person for having epilepsy; we treat it and keep others safe. Sapolsky notes that epilepsy was once blamed on witchcraft or sin, and schizophrenia on bad mothering, and that understanding the biology made the world kinder. He expects the same to happen, gradually, for behavior we now call evil.

Practically, this points toward prevention and quarantine rather than retribution. Dangerous people may still need to be kept from harming others, the way we isolate someone with a contagious disease, but without the extra layer of cruelty that comes from thinking they deserve to suffer. Derk Pereboom developed this "quarantine model" in detail.

The hardest objection is that most philosophers don't accept the starting point. Compatibilists, like Daniel Dennett, agree the world is causal but argue that "free will worth wanting" means acting from your own reasons without coercion, and that this is real. Sapolsky's reply is that it simply relabels the problem: your reasons, too, came from somewhere you didn't choose.

**Key figure.** Robert Sapolsky.

**Key texts.** *Behave: The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst* (2017); *Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will* (2023).

**Origins and context.** Sapolsky spent decades studying baboons in Kenya and stress in humans, documenting how hormones, childhood adversity, and culture shape behavior. *Determined* appeared amid debates over criminal justice, addiction, and mental illness, arguing these should be treated more like medical conditions than moral failures.

**Other voices.** Spinoza; Clarence Darrow, the lawyer who argued in the 1924 Leopold and Loeb case that environment and heredity shaped his clients; Derk Pereboom, *Living Without Free Will* (2001).

**Related ideas.** Hard determinism; the medical model of addiction; restorative justice.

**Pros.**
- Undercuts hatred and revenge.
- Points toward prevention rather than punishment.
- Offers self-forgiveness.

**Cons.**
- Most philosophers favor compatibilism, which keeps responsibility.
- Can leave some people feeling powerless.
- Society still needs some way to hold people accountable.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If nobody has free will, can anyone be blamed for anything?
>
> **Neuroscientist:** Blamed in the sense of deserving to suffer? I don't think so. Held responsible, in the sense of being stopped or helped to change? Absolutely.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that let criminals off the hook?
>
> **Neuroscientist:** It changes the hook. We still protect people from someone dangerous, the way we isolate a contagious patient. We just stop adding suffering for its own sake.
>
> **Ari:** But I *feel* like I choose things.
>
> **Neuroscientist:** You do choose, in the sense that your brain weighs options. But you didn't choose the brain doing the weighing, or the life that shaped it.
>
> **Ari:** That's a little depressing.
>
> **Neuroscientist:** I find it freeing. It's very hard to hate someone once you can see everything that made them. And it's easier to forgive yourself too.

**Try it.**

- Think of someone you resent and list everything outside their control that shaped them: their upbringing, health, stresses and luck.
- Next time you fail at a habit, change your environment instead of blaming your willpower.

## 12. Care Ethics

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 310 · Believed: 320 · Likely true: 35%*

**In summary.** Morality grows from relationships of care, like those between parent and child, rather than from abstract rules or calculations. The moral question is how to respond to the needs of particular people in particular situations.

**In depth.**

Care ethics begins with an ordinary fact that most moral theories skip over: every human life starts in total dependence. Nobody reaches adulthood without years of someone feeding, cleaning, comforting and teaching them, and most of us will need that care again when we are ill or old. Carol Gilligan argued that morality built on this experience looks different from morality built on abstract rights. Her study participants, many of them women facing real decisions like whether to end a pregnancy, reasoned in terms of responsibility and relationship: "who will be hurt, and how can I keep connection?" rather than "whose rights win?"

Nel Noddings described caring as having two sides. The one-caring pays close, receptive attention to what the other person actually needs (she called it *engrossment*), and the cared-for responds, completing the relationship. Good care is not imposing what you think is good for someone; it's paying attention until you understand what they need. Virginia Held and Joan Tronto later extended care ethics from families to politics, arguing that societies should be judged partly by how well they organize care for children, the sick, the disabled and the elderly, and by whether caregivers, often underpaid women, are valued.

A useful way to see the difference: faced with a lonely, struggling neighbour, a utilitarian might ask how to maximize welfare overall, and a Kantian what duty requires. A care ethicist asks what *this* person needs from *you*, given your relationship and what you can actually give, and treats attentiveness itself as a moral skill.

The main criticism is partiality. If morality grows from close relationships, it can neglect strangers far away who need far more. Care ethicists reply that caring about distant people is learned *through* caring about near ones, and that institutions can carry care beyond the circle of family.

**Key figures.** Carol Gilligan (born 1936); Nel Noddings (1929–2022).

**Key texts.** Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982); Noddings's *Caring* (1984); Virginia Held's *The Ethics of Care* (2006).

**Origins and context.** Gilligan, a psychologist, challenged her colleague Lawrence Kohlberg's theory of moral development, which ranked justice-based reasoning highest and placed many women lower. She argued women's emphasis on relationships was a different moral voice, not a lesser one. The work emerged with second-wave feminism.

**Other voices.** Joan Tronto, *Moral Boundaries* (1993); Eva Feder Kittay on dependency; Confucian ethics, which also centers relationships.

**Related ideas.** Virtue ethics; Ubuntu ("I am because we are"); feminist ethics.

**Pros.**
- Honors the real work of caregiving.
- Attends to context and emotion.
- Makes morality personal and practical.

**Cons.**
- Can favor those close to us over distant strangers in greater need.
- Offers less guidance for large-scale policy.
- Critics argue it risks reinforcing gender stereotypes.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** What's wrong with just following good rules?
>
> **Care ethicist:** Rules are fine, but they can't tell you what your grandmother needs this afternoon. Only paying attention to her can.
>
> **Ari:** So morality is about relationships, not principles?
>
> **Care ethicist:** It starts there. Every one of us was kept alive as a baby by someone's care. Morality grows out of that.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that favour family over strangers in greater need?
>
> **Care ethicist:** It can, and that's a fair worry. But people who learn to care well up close are usually better at caring about strangers too, and good institutions can extend care much further than any one family.
>
> **Ari:** What does a "good carer" look like, then?
>
> **Care ethicist:** Someone who listens before helping, notices what's actually needed, and doesn't burn themselves out, because caring for yourself is part of being able to care at all.

**Try it.**

- Ask someone close to you, "What would actually help you this week?" and do that rather than what you assumed.
- Thank a caregiver in your life specifically for something they did.

## 13. Gradual Compassion

*Group: Religious message · Told: 310 · Believed: 340 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Suffering arises from craving; it can end through a gradual path of ethical conduct, mental discipline, and wisdom. Compassion for all beings is cultivated step by step.

**In depth.**

The Buddha's teaching is framed like a medical diagnosis, in the Four Noble Truths. Life involves *dukkha*: suffering, or more precisely unsatisfactoriness, the sense that things never quite settle. Its cause is *tanha*, craving: grasping for pleasant things to stay, pushing unpleasant things away, and clinging to a fixed sense of self. It can end: the cessation of craving is *nirvana*. And there is a path to that end, the Noble Eightfold Path, grouped into ethical conduct (right speech, action and livelihood), mental discipline (right effort, mindfulness and concentration) and wisdom (right view and intention).

The path is explicitly gradual. Laypeople begin with the Five Precepts: not killing, not stealing, no sexual misconduct, no false speech, and no intoxicants that cloud the mind. Each precept is phrased as a training rule ("I undertake the training to abstain from…") rather than a commandment, which captures the spirit: practice, not perfection.

Compassion grows the same way. The four *brahmaviharas*, or "divine abodes," are loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy in others' happiness, and equanimity. They are cultivated deliberately in meditation, often starting with yourself and widening the circle outward. In Mahayana Buddhism the ideal becomes the bodhisattva, who vows to delay their own liberation until all beings are freed, a picture of compassion without limit.

Modern psychology has borrowed heavily from this toolkit. Mindfulness-based stress reduction and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, the latter shown to reduce relapse in recurrent depression, use Buddhist techniques stripped of religious claims. The main misreading of Buddhism is that non-attachment means not caring. Teachers emphasize the opposite: letting go of grasping frees attention to care for others more clearly.

**Key figure.** Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha (c. 5th century BCE).

**Key texts.** The Pali Canon; Shantideva's *Bodhicaryavatara* (8th century), a classic guide to compassion.

**Origins and context.** Born a prince in what is now Nepal, Siddhartha left luxury after encountering sickness, old age, and death. After years of extreme asceticism failed, he found a "middle way" and taught the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path during an era of spiritual experimentation in India.

**Other voices.** Thich Nhat Hanh; the 14th Dalai Lama; Jon Kabat-Zinn, who adapted mindfulness for medicine (MBSR, 1979).

**Related ideas.** Stoicism; mindfulness; the author's relative deprivation principle.

**Pros.**
- Incremental path avoids all-or-nothing guilt.
- Mindfulness practices have research support for stress reduction.
- Extends compassion to all beings.

**Cons.**
- Ultimate goal (enlightenment) is very demanding.
- Can be misread as detachment from the world.
- Monastic emphasis isn't practical for many.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Buddhism says life is suffering. That sounds bleak.
>
> **Monk:** It says life involves suffering, and then it says suffering can end. That's the hopeful part.
>
> **Ari:** End how?
>
> **Monk:** Most of our suffering comes from craving: wanting things to be other than they are, wanting good moments to last forever. Loosen that grip and much of the suffering loosens with it.
>
> **Ari:** If I stop craving, won't I stop caring too?
>
> **Monk:** People fear that, but it's the reverse. When you're not busy grasping for yourself, you have more attention left for others.
>
> **Ari:** I can't meditate for hours a day.
>
> **Monk:** Nobody starts there. Keep one precept well. Sit for five minutes. Wish one difficult person well. The path is gradual on purpose.

**Try it.**

- Sit quietly for five minutes, noticing your breath, and gently return whenever your mind wanders.
- Choose one of the Five Precepts, such as no false speech, and keep it carefully for a week.
- When you catch yourself craving something, name it ("wanting") and watch the feeling rise and pass.

## 14. Classical Liberalism

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 310 · Believed: 320 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Government exists to protect individual rights (life, liberty, property) under the same rules for everyone.

**In depth.**

Classical liberalism rests on a few linked ideas. Individuals have natural rights to life, liberty and property that exist before any government. Governments are created by people to protect those rights, and they are legitimate only with the consent of the governed. Power must be limited by law that applies equally to everyone, rulers included, and divided so no one person or body can dominate. Within those protections, people should be free to speak, worship, trade and live as they choose, so long as they don't harm others.

John Stuart Mill gave the last point its classic form in the "harm principle": the only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over anyone against their will is to prevent harm to others. Your own good, physical or moral, isn't enough reason. Mill also argued for free speech on practical grounds: if an opinion is right, silencing it denies us the truth; if wrong, debating it strengthens our understanding of why the truth is true.

Adam Smith added the economic side. Free exchange, he argued, lets people meet each other's needs without central direction, and protecting property and contracts gives everyone a reason to produce. But Smith was also a moral philosopher who wrote that sympathy for others is central to human nature, and he warned that merchants often conspire against the public. Classical liberalism was a revolt against kings, monopolies and established churches as much as a defence of markets.

Its achievements are hard to overstate: constitutional government, religious toleration, free speech, the abolition of legal privileges by birth. Its critics argue that equal rules on paper can coexist with very unequal chances in practice, and that early liberals often excluded women, workers without property and enslaved people from the rights they proclaimed. Modern debates between conservatives, progressives and libertarians are largely arguments within the liberal tradition about how to apply its principles.

**Key figure.** John Locke (1632–1704).

**Key texts.** Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1689); Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776); John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* (1859).

**Origins and context.** Locke wrote around England's Glorious Revolution (1688), arguing rulers govern by consent and can be removed if they violate rights. These ideas shaped the American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the Enlightenment's push against absolute monarchy and religious persecution.

**Other voices.** Montesquieu (separation of powers); Thomas Jefferson; Friedrich Hayek; Steven Pinker, *Enlightenment Now* (2018).

**Related ideas.** Rule of law; free speech; constitutional democracy.

**Pros.**
- Unifying: equal rules for all.
- Foundation of modern democracies and human rights.
- Achievable and broadly accepted.

**Cons.**
- Formal equality can overlook unequal starting points.
- Early liberals often excluded women, the poor, and enslaved people.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** What's the one-sentence version of classical liberalism?
>
> **Liberal:** Government exists to protect your rights, and it should do little else.
>
> **Ari:** Which rights?
>
> **Liberal:** Life, liberty and property, in Locke's words. Speaking, worshipping, trading and living your own way, as long as you don't harm others.
>
> **Ari:** Who decides what counts as harm?
>
> **Liberal:** That's the hard part, and it's why we argue about it in parliaments and courts rather than letting one ruler decide.
>
> **Ari:** Equal rules sound nice, but people don't start equal.
>
> **Liberal:** True, and many modern liberals would add a safety net for exactly that reason. But equal rules are what protect the weak from the strong. Before them, your rights depended on who your father was.

**Try it.**

- Pick an opinion you strongly disagree with and write down the best argument for it, as Mill recommended.
- Next time you want a rule to restrict something, ask whether it prevents harm to others or only something you dislike.

## 15. Bad as a Learning Signal

*Group: Your principle · Told: 300 · Believed: 320 · Likely true: 60%*

**In summary.** "Bad" is a tool that individuals and societies use to communicate and teach what they value, much as one trains a dog or a group. It works, but at a cost: it brings shame, anger, and judgment. Understood as a signal rather than a verdict, it can be heard, learned from, and released.

**In depth.**

This principle starts from a question: what is the word "bad" for? Its answer is that "bad" is a tool individuals and societies use to teach what they value. Disapproval, blame and shame shape behaviour, much as a dog learns from a firm "no" or a group learns its norms from who gets criticized. Evolutionary research supports the idea: Robert Trivers argued that emotions like guilt, gratitude and moral outrage evolved to regulate cooperation, rewarding those who give and punishing those who cheat.

Seeing "bad" this way doesn't make it useless. The signal carries information: when someone calls what you did bad, they're telling you what they or their community care about. That's worth hearing. What the principle rejects is the extra step of treating the signal as a verdict on what a person *is*. June Tangney's research shows why this matters: guilt ("I did a bad thing") tends to lead to apology and repair, while shame ("I am bad") tends to lead to hiding, defensiveness and anger. The signal works best when it's received as guilt and released before it hardens into shame.

So the practice has three steps. Hear the signal, without dismissing it. Learn from it: what value was at stake, and what would honour it next time? Then release it: the lesson stays, the sting doesn't.

The principle needs care in one place. People who have been harmed often need the word "bad" to name what happened to them. Reframing their pain too quickly can feel like minimizing it. The principle applies most cleanly to how we treat our own mistakes and how we hold people accountable, not to how we respond to someone describing harm done to them.

**Key figures.** Robert Trivers (born 1943), who proposed that emotions like guilt, gratitude, and moral outrage evolved to regulate cooperation; Jonathan Haidt (born 1963), who mapped the moral emotions.

**Key texts.** Trivers's paper "The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism" (1971); Haidt's chapter "The Moral Emotions" (2003) and book *The Righteous Mind* (2012).

**Origins and context.** Adam Smith's *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) already treated moral judgment as arising from social feeling. In the twentieth century, behaviorists studied how punishment shapes behavior, and evolutionary biologists began asking why moral emotions exist at all. The answer that emerged: they help groups enforce norms and cooperate.

**Other voices.** Charles Darwin, *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872); Émile Durkheim, who argued that punishing deviance reinforces shared norms; June Tangney, whose research distinguishes guilt ("I did a bad thing"), which tends to motivate repair, from shame ("I am bad"), which tends to produce defensiveness.

**Related ideas.** Restorative justice; Nietzsche's genealogy of morals, which also treated "good" and "evil" as products of social history; operant conditioning.

**Pros.**
- Respects why people use the word "bad" while removing its sting.
- Encourages learning from criticism without carrying lasting shame.
- Fits well-supported research on moral emotions.

**Cons.**
- Victims may need the word "bad" to name what happened to them; reframing it too quickly can feel invalidating.
- Explaining the function of moral judgment doesn't settle whether some acts are wrong regardless of signals.

**Worth knowing.** The shortest form: *"Bad is a signal, not a verdict."*

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Someone called what I did "bad" and it's eating me up.
>
> **Author:** What were they telling you they care about?
>
> **Ari:** I guess that I let the team down.
>
> **Author:** Then that's the signal: they value reliability. That's useful to know.
>
> **Ari:** So I should feel bad?
>
> **Author:** Feel it enough to learn. Then let it go. Bad is a signal, not a verdict. It's about what you did, not who you are.
>
> **Ari:** And if someone hurt me? Can't I call that bad?
>
> **Author:** Of course. When you've been harmed, the word protects you and warns others. That's the signal doing its job.

**Try it.**

- Next time you're criticized, write down the value behind the criticism, one thing you'll do differently, and then close the notebook.

## 16. Utilitarianism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 300 · Believed: 310 · Likely true: 20%*

**In summary.** The right action produces the greatest happiness and least suffering for everyone affected, each counting equally. Not wealth, not rules, but well-being is what matters.

**In depth.**

Bentham's starting point was that pain and pleasure are the "two sovereign masters" that govern what we do and what we ought to do. An action is right insofar as it increases happiness and reduces suffering, counting everyone affected equally: "each to count for one, and none for more than one." He even proposed a "felicific calculus" to weigh pleasures and pains by intensity, duration, certainty, nearness and how many people they reach.

Mill softened and deepened the theory. He argued that some pleasures are higher than others: those of the intellect, imagination and moral feeling outrank mere sensation, because anyone who has experienced both prefers them. "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." He also defended rights and justice as rules that, on the whole, produce the most happiness.

That move points to an important split. *Act* utilitarianism judges each action by its consequences. *Rule* utilitarianism judges actions by whether they follow rules that produce the best results when generally followed, such as "don't punish the innocent" and "keep promises." Many famous objections target the first version. The classic one is a surgeon who could kill one healthy patient to give organs to five dying ones. Act utilitarianism seems to approve; rule utilitarians and most actual utilitarians reply that a world where doctors did this would be catastrophically worse, since nobody would trust hospitals.

Utilitarianism's record in practice is striking: Bentham argued for decriminalizing homosexuality and for animal welfare in the eighteenth century, and Mill for women's suffrage. Today it underlies cost-benefit analysis, public-health decisions and effective altruism. Its persistent difficulty is measurement: happiness doesn't come in units, and comparing one person's joy with another's suffering requires judgment no formula can remove.

**Key figures.** Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832); John Stuart Mill (1806–1873).

**Key texts.** Bentham's *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789); Mill's *Utilitarianism* (1863).

**Origins and context.** Bentham wrote during the Enlightenment and the age of revolutions, aiming to reform Britain's harsh laws on rational grounds. He campaigned against cruel punishments and for animal welfare, famously asking of animals whether they can suffer rather than whether they can reason. Mill refined the theory, arguing that some pleasures (intellectual, moral) are higher than others.

**Other voices.** Harriet Taylor Mill; Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics* (1874); Peter Singer.

**Related ideas.** Consequentialism (its parent family); effective altruism; welfare economics.

**Pros.**
- Impartial: everyone's suffering counts, including animals'.
- Drove real reforms: abolition of cruel punishments, women's suffrage (Mill), animal welfare.
- Simple guiding question.

**Cons.**
- Can justify sacrificing individuals for the majority.
- Measuring and comparing happiness is hard.
- Its demandingness can produce guilt.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Utilitarianism means doing whatever makes the most people happy?
>
> **Utilitarian:** Close. Whatever produces the most well-being and least suffering, counting everyone equally, including animals.
>
> **Ari:** So if harvesting one person's organs saved five, you'd do it?
>
> **Utilitarian:** Think about the consequences properly. If doctors did that, nobody would go to a hospital again. The world would be far worse. Good utilitarians keep rules like "don't kill patients" because those rules produce the most good.
>
> **Ari:** Then how is it different from ordinary morality?
>
> **Utilitarian:** It tells you *why* the rules matter, and it lets you question rules that cause needless suffering. That's how Bentham ended up arguing against cruel punishments and for animals centuries early.
>
> **Ari:** How do you measure happiness, though?
>
> **Utilitarian:** Imperfectly. But refusing to estimate doesn't make the suffering go away. Rough measuring beats ignoring it.

**Try it.**

- Before a decision this week, list everyone it affects and roughly how much, including people and animals you won't see.
- Look for one rule you follow that causes more suffering than it prevents, and ask whether it's worth keeping.

## 17. Stoicism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 300 · Believed: 320 · Likely true: 45%*

**In summary.** Distinguish what you control (your judgments and actions) from what you don't (everything else). Live by virtue (wisdom, courage, justice, temperance), accept fate calmly, and treat all humans as fellow citizens of the world.

**In depth.**

The heart of Stoicism is the opening line of Epictetus's *Enchiridion*: some things are within our power and some are not. Within our power are our judgments, intentions, desires and actions. Outside it are our body, reputation, wealth, other people and events. Suffering, the Stoics argued, comes from staking our happiness on the second category. Epictetus, who had been enslaved and was lame, knew how little of the outer world anyone controls.

From this follows the Stoic view of emotions. They are not irrational storms but the result of judgments. "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the opinions they have of things." If you judge an insult to be a catastrophe, you'll feel rage; if you judge it as information about the insulter, you won't. Stoics weren't trying to feel nothing. They aimed to replace destructive passions like anger and terror with good feelings like joy, caution and goodwill, by correcting false judgments.

The only true good, for Stoics, is virtue: wisdom, courage, justice and temperance. Health, wealth and fame are "preferred indifferents": reasonable to pursue, but not what makes a life good. That is why Marcus Aurelius, the most powerful man in the Roman world, wrote reminders to himself to stay humble, patient and just. Stoicism also had a strong social side: the Stoics called themselves citizens of the world (*kosmopolitai*) and saw duty to others as part of living according to nature.

Practical exercises filled their writing: imagining losses in advance (*premeditatio malorum*) so they hurt less and life is appreciated more; reviewing your day each evening; and taking a "view from above," seeing your problems from a cosmic distance. Cognitive behavioural therapy borrowed the central technique directly: identify the judgment behind a feeling, test whether it's accurate, and replace it.

**Key figures.** Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BCE), founder; Epictetus (c. 50–135); Seneca (c. 4 BCE–65 CE); Marcus Aurelius (121–180).

**Key texts.** Epictetus's *Enchiridion*; Seneca's *Letters*; Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*.

**Origins and context.** Zeno taught at a painted porch (*stoa*) in Athens around 300 BCE, after Alexander's empire fractured and city-states lost independence. A philosophy of inner freedom suited a world where outer control was slipping. Its greatest Roman voices included a former slave (Epictetus) and an emperor (Marcus Aurelius).

**Other voices.** Cicero; Aaron Beck and Albert Ellis, who credited Epictetus as an inspiration for cognitive therapy; Ryan Holiday's popular books.

**Related ideas.** Buddhism (non-attachment); cognitive behavioral therapy; the Serenity Prayer.

**Pros.**
- Builds resilience and calm.
- Emphasizes duty to others, not just self-help.
- Underlies proven therapies.

**Cons.**
- Can be misread as suppressing emotion.
- Acceptance of fate may discourage fighting fixable injustice.
- Its ancient physics and theology are largely abandoned.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** I got passed over for a promotion and I'm furious.
>
> **Stoic:** What exactly are you angry about?
>
> **Ari:** That they chose someone worse than me!
>
> **Stoic:** Their choice was never in your control. What is?
>
> **Ari:** I guess how I respond, and how I work from here.
>
> **Stoic:** Then put your energy there. The anger comes from the judgment that this is a disaster. Is it?
>
> **Ari:** Not a disaster. Disappointing.
>
> **Stoic:** Good, that's a more accurate judgment, and you'll notice it hurts less. Stoics don't tell you to feel nothing. They tell you to see clearly.
>
> **Ari:** Isn't that just accepting unfairness?
>
> **Stoic:** No. Justice is one of our four virtues. If it was unfair, speak up calmly and act. Just don't hand your peace of mind to people you can't control.

**Try it.**

- When something upsets you, sort it into "up to me" and "not up to me," and act only on the first.
- Each evening, ask Seneca's questions: what did I do well today, what badly, and what will I do better?
- Spend one minute imagining losing something you value, then go and appreciate it.

## 18. Virtue Ethics

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 300 · Believed: 310 · Likely true: 37%*

**In summary.** Instead of asking "what rule should I follow?" or "what outcome is best?", ask "what kind of person should I be?" Build character traits like courage, honesty, and generosity through practice, aiming at a flourishing life (*eudaimonia*).

**In depth.**

Aristotle began with a question every other question depends on: what is the good life for a human being? His answer was *eudaimonia*, usually translated "flourishing." It is not a feeling of happiness but a whole life lived well, the way a healthy tree flourishes by fully being a tree. Humans flourish by exercising what is distinctive in us, reason, in action and in community, over a complete life.

Virtues are the stable traits of character that make this possible. Aristotle's famous "doctrine of the mean" holds that each virtue sits between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage lies between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between stinginess and extravagance, honesty between deceit and tactlessness. The mean isn't a fixed midpoint; it is what's right for this person, at this time, toward these people. Finding it takes *phronesis*, practical wisdom: the skill of seeing what a situation calls for.

Virtues are built like skills. "We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." At first you may need to force yourself; with practice, good action becomes natural and even pleasant. That is why virtue ethics cares so much about habits, role models and upbringing. One practical heuristic modern virtue ethicists offer: in a hard case, ask what a person of good character, someone you admire, would do.

After centuries in the shadow of rule-based and outcome-based theories, virtue ethics revived when Elizabeth Anscombe argued in 1958 that modern moral philosophy had lost touch with psychology, and Alasdair MacIntyre argued in *After Virtue* (1981) that morality makes sense only inside traditions and practices with shared goods. Its strength is that it describes the whole moral life, not just dilemmas. Its weakness is guidance: "do what the virtuous person would do" can be hard to apply when you don't know what that is.

**Key figure.** Aristotle (384–322 BCE).

**Key text.** *Nicomachean Ethics* (c. 340 BCE).

**Origins and context.** Aristotle, Plato's student and Alexander the Great's tutor, observed that virtues are habits found between extremes: courage lies between cowardice and recklessness. Virtue ethics faded in modern philosophy until Elizabeth Anscombe's 1958 essay "Modern Moral Philosophy" urged a return to it.

**Other voices.** Thomas Aquinas; Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981); Philippa Foot; Rosalind Hursthouse; Confucius, whose ethics share much with Aristotle's.

**Related ideas.** Character education; positive psychology's character strengths; Stoicism.

**Pros.**
- Practical and holistic: covers the whole life, not just dilemmas.
- Recognizes moral growth through habit.
- About 37% of philosophers favored it in the 2020 PhilPapers survey, the largest share.

**Cons.**
- Gives vague guidance in hard cases.
- Which traits count as virtues varies by culture.
- Aristotle's original version excluded women and slaves from full virtue.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Other theories give me a rule or a calculation. What does virtue ethics give me?
>
> **Aristotelian:** A different question. Not "what should I do?" but "who should I become?"
>
> **Ari:** How does that help in a tough moment?
>
> **Aristotelian:** Ask what a person of good character would do here: honest, but not cruel; brave, but not reckless. And ask who you're becoming by choosing.
>
> **Ari:** What if I'm not that person yet?
>
> **Aristotelian:** Nobody starts there. Virtues are habits. You become generous by giving, brave by facing small fears. It feels forced at first and natural later.
>
> **Ari:** And the goal?
>
> **Aristotelian:** A flourishing life: one that's good to live and good for the people around you, not just a pleasant one.

**Try it.**

- Pick one virtue you want more of, such as patience or courage, and practise one small act of it each day this week.
- In your next hard decision, ask what someone you deeply admire would do.

## 19. Consequentialism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 300 · Believed: 310 · Likely true: 31%*

**In summary.** The rightness of an action depends entirely on its consequences. Utilitarianism is the best-known version, but consequentialists can value outcomes other than happiness, such as knowledge, rights fulfilled, or preference satisfaction.

**In depth.**

Consequentialism is the family of views holding that the moral quality of an action depends only on what it brings about. Utilitarianism is its most famous member, valuing happiness or well-being. Other consequentialists count other goods: satisfied preferences, knowledge, beauty, rights respected, or a list of several goods together. What unites them is the shape of the theory: identify what's good, then do what produces the most of it.

This makes consequentialism *agent-neutral*. My child's suffering counts no more, morally, than a stranger's, and breaking a promise isn't wrong in itself, only if it makes things worse. Critics find that too cold; supporters find it honest. Many consequentialists distinguish the *criterion* of right action (best consequences) from a good *decision procedure*: in daily life, they say, you usually do best by keeping promises, loving your family and following sensible rules, because constant calculating backfires.

Derek Parfit pushed consequentialist thinking into strange and important territory. In *Reasons and Persons* he explored how to weigh future generations, and found that seemingly obvious principles lead to the "Repugnant Conclusion": that a vast population of lives barely worth living could be better than a smaller, very happy one. Population ethics remains one of philosophy's hardest open problems, and it matters for questions like climate change and existential risk.

Alastair Norcross's scalar consequentialism is closest to this book's framing. He argues that "right" and "wrong" are arbitrary cutoffs on a continuous scale; what's real is that some actions are better or worse than others, by degrees.

**Key figures.** Elizabeth Anscombe coined the term in 1958 (as a critic); Henry Sidgwick and Derek Parfit developed it.

**Key texts.** Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics* (1874); Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984).

**Origins and context.** Ancient China had an early version: Mozi (c. 470–391 BCE) judged practices by whether they benefited the people. Modern consequentialism grew from British utilitarianism and became a dominant framework in twentieth-century analytic philosophy and in policy analysis.

**Other voices.** Peter Railton; Shelly Kagan; Alastair Norcross, whose scalar version abandons "right" and "wrong" for "better" and "worse."

**Related ideas.** Utilitarianism; cost-benefit analysis; effective altruism.

**Pros.**
- Focuses on what actually happens to people.
- Flexible and widely used in policy.
- Encourages thinking about long-term effects.

**Cons.**
- Can justify terrible means for good ends.
- Consequences are hard to predict.
- About 31% of philosophers favored it in 2020.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** So the ends justify the means?
>
> **Consequentialist:** The total consequences do, and the means are part of the consequences. Lying, coercion and broken trust all have costs that count.
>
> **Ari:** Then why not lie when it helps?
>
> **Consequentialist:** Usually it doesn't help, once you count everything. Trust is precious, and people who lie whenever it seems useful stop being trusted.
>
> **Ari:** Do you calculate every decision?
>
> **Consequentialist:** No, that would be exhausting and error-prone. I follow good rules and habits, and I save the careful thinking for big choices or for when the rules clearly cause harm.
>
> **Ari:** What's the hardest part of your view?
>
> **Consequentialist:** Predicting outcomes, and comparing very different kinds of good. And future generations: how much should people who don't exist yet count? Nobody has a fully satisfying answer.

**Try it.**

- For a choice you're facing, write down its likely effects one week, one year and ten years out.
- Identify a rule you follow, and ask what consequences it protects against.

## 20. Turn the Other Cheek

*Group: Strategy · Told: 300 · Believed: 320 · Likely true: not assessed (a strategy)*

**In summary.** When someone wrongs you, do not retaliate. Respond to aggression with patience and goodwill, breaking the cycle of revenge.

**In depth.**

The verse reads: "If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to them the other also." In Jesus' world, a strike on the right cheek from a right-handed person meant a backhand: not a fight between equals but an insult from a superior to an inferior, a master to a servant. Walter Wink argued that turning the other cheek was therefore a quiet act of defiance. It refused to accept humiliation while also refusing to strike back, and it forced the aggressor either to stop or to hit with an open palm, which treated the victim as an equal. Wink read the neighbouring teachings, giving your cloak as well as your coat and going a second mile, the same way: creative non-retaliation that exposes injustice.

Whatever the original meaning, the practical power of non-retaliation is well documented. Retaliation produces counter-retaliation; feuds can last generations. A response that absorbs a blow without returning it breaks the cycle, and in public settings it can shift observers' sympathy decisively. Gandhi built *satyagraha*, "truth-force," on this principle, and Martin Luther King Jr. trained civil-rights activists to sit calmly at segregated lunch counters while being abused, which made the injustice visible to the whole country.

Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan's study of over 300 resistance campaigns from 1900 to 2006 found that nonviolent ones succeeded about twice as often as violent ones, largely because they drew far broader participation and could win over defectors from the other side.

The strategy has real limits, which is why its score is high but not top. It depends on an audience whose conscience can be reached, it can be exploited by people who face no consequences, and it has been misused to tell abuse victims to endure. Non-retaliation is not the same as non-resistance: leaving, seeking protection, and pursuing justice all remain compatible with it.

**Key figure.** Jesus of Nazareth (c. 4 BCE–c. 30 CE).

**Key text.** The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:38–39; Luke 6:29).

**Origins and context.** Jesus taught in Roman-occupied Judea, where resentment of the occupiers ran high and violent uprisings were brewing. Directly contrasting the older "eye for an eye," he urged non-retaliation. Biblical scholar Walter Wink argued in *Engaging the Powers* (1992) that the instruction was subtly defiant: turning the other cheek forced a backhanding superior either to stop or to strike as an equal.

**Other voices.** Leo Tolstoy, *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894); Mohandas Gandhi, who developed *satyagraha* (truth-force) in South Africa from 1906; Martin Luther King Jr., who led the Montgomery bus boycott in 1955.

**Related ideas.** Nonviolent resistance; pacifism; the Buddhist and Jain principle of nonharm.

**Pros.**
- Breaks cycles of retaliation.
- As organized nonviolence, it has toppled regimes; Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan found nonviolent campaigns succeeded about twice as often as violent ones (1900–2006).
- Preserves the victim's dignity and moral high ground.

**Cons.**
- Risky against exploiters when you lack protection, which fits the author's point that a safety net makes higher goodness possible.
- Can be misused to tell victims to endure abuse.
- Works best with an audience whose conscience can be reached.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If someone hits me, I'm supposed to just let them do it again?
>
> **Activist:** Not quite. Don't hit back, but don't give in either. Stand there, unafraid, and make them see what they're doing.
>
> **Ari:** And that works?
>
> **Activist:** Far more often than you'd think. When the lunch-counter protesters sat calmly while being abused, the whole country saw who was in the wrong.
>
> **Ari:** What about a bully who doesn't care who's watching?
>
> **Activist:** Then protect yourself. Leave, get help, call the authorities. Turning the other cheek was never meant to keep anyone in an abusive situation.
>
> **Ari:** So what's the core of it?
>
> **Activist:** Refusing to let someone else's violence decide your behaviour. Revenge keeps the cycle going; you can be the one who ends it.

**Try it.**

- The next time someone is rude to you, respond calmly and kindly once, and notice what happens.
- Think of a feud in your life, big or small, and consider one step that would break the cycle without surrendering.

## 21. Social Democracy

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 300 · Believed: 310 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Within a market economy and democracy, government should cushion bad luck through public services and safety nets, so circumstances matter less.

**In depth.**

Social democracy grew out of a split within nineteenth-century socialism. Revolutionary Marxists expected capitalism to collapse and wanted to overthrow it; Eduard Bernstein looked at the evidence and saw workers' lives improving through unions, voting and reform. He argued that socialism should be pursued gradually and democratically, keeping the market economy but taming it. "The movement is everything," he wrote; the final goal mattered less than steady progress.

The model that emerged in the twentieth century, especially in the Nordic countries, combines three things: a competitive market economy that produces wealth; strong labour unions that bargain over wages across whole industries; and a universal welfare state funded by high taxes, providing healthcare, education, childcare, pensions and unemployment insurance to everyone, not just the poor. Universality matters politically: when the middle class benefits too, the programmes stay popular. Denmark adds "flexicurity": employers can hire and fire fairly easily, but displaced workers get generous support and retraining.

In this book's terms, social democracy is an attempt to pave the road. If goodness is distance travelled, and some people walk through mud while others walk on pavement, a safety net reduces how much of the outcome depends on luck: who your parents were, whether you got sick, whether your industry collapsed.

Its critics raise real questions. High taxes may reduce incentives to work and invest; generous benefits may create dependency; ageing populations strain the budget. Supporters point out that Nordic countries combine high taxes with high employment, strong economies and some of the highest life-satisfaction scores in the world, though some of that success may also reflect high social trust and small, cohesive populations.

**Key figure.** Eduard Bernstein (1850–1932).

**Key texts.** Bernstein's *Evolutionary Socialism* (1899); the Beveridge Report (1942); Gøsta Esping-Andersen's *The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism* (1990).

## 22. Free-Market Capitalism

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 300 · Believed: 300 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Government should protect property and contracts and otherwise let voluntary exchange work. You prosper by serving other people's needs.

**In depth.**

Adam Smith's central insight was that markets coordinate millions of people without anyone being in charge. "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." Each of them serves you because it serves them, and the result, as if by an "invisible hand," is that people's needs get met far more reliably than any planner could arrange. Smith also showed how the division of labour multiplies productivity: in his famous pin factory, ten workers each doing one step could make about 48,000 pins a day, where one person doing every step might make twenty.

Friedrich Hayek added a deeper reason. The knowledge needed to run an economy is scattered among millions of people: what's scarce in one town, what a farmer knows about her land, what a shopkeeper notices about customers. Prices summarize all that knowledge in a single number that anyone can act on. No central authority could ever gather it, which is why planned economies kept producing shortages and surpluses. Milton Friedman linked economic freedom to political freedom, arguing that when the state controls your livelihood, it controls you.

The case rests heavily on results. Since market economies spread in the late twentieth century, especially in China and India, extreme poverty has fallen faster than at any time in history. Competition drives innovation, and voluntary exchange means both sides gain.

The main objections are well known. Markets ignore costs that fall on third parties, like pollution and climate change, unless rules make them pay. They can concentrate wealth and power, and inequality can undermine democracy and opportunity. Financial markets can crash, as in 2008. Most economists today support markets *plus* rules: competition law, environmental pricing and some redistribution. The disagreement is mainly about how much.

**Key figure.** Adam Smith (1723–1790).

**Key texts.** *The Wealth of Nations* (1776); Friedrich Hayek's *The Road to Serfdom* (1944); Milton Friedman's *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962).

**Origins and context.** Smith wrote at the dawn of the Industrial Revolution, arguing that markets coordinate self-interest toward the common good, as if by an "invisible hand." Hayek and Friedman revived free-market ideas against mid-twentieth-century central planning.

**Other voices.** Joseph Schumpeter ("creative destruction"); Deirdre McCloskey, *Bourgeois Dignity* (2010).

**Related ideas.** Entrepreneurship; globalization; classical liberalism.

**Pros.**
- Market economies drove historic wealth growth; World Bank figures show extreme poverty fell from over a third of humanity in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019.
- Rewards effort and innovation.

**Cons.**
- "You earned your outcome" can shame the poor and breed contempt.
- Inequality, externalities like pollution, and financial crises (2008) remain major concerns; see Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013).

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Capitalism is just greed, isn't it?
>
> **Economist:** Self-interest, channelled. The baker gets rich only by baking bread people want to buy. The market rewards serving others.
>
> **Ari:** Then why is there so much inequality?
>
> **Economist:** Markets reward value created, and luck, and sometimes power. That's why most of us want competition rules and a safety net alongside them.
>
> **Ari:** Wouldn't planning be fairer?
>
> **Economist:** It's been tried. No planner can know what millions of people need each day. Prices carry that information automatically. Planned economies kept running short of bread.
>
> **Ari:** What about pollution?
>
> **Economist:** A real market failure. The polluter doesn't pay the cost, so we need rules or a price on carbon. Markets work best when prices tell the truth.

**Try it.**

- Pick one everyday object near you and trace how many people, in how many countries, helped make it reach you.
- Ask, for one thing you buy, whether its price includes the harm it causes, and what it would cost if it did.

## 23. Diversity of Philosophy

*Group: Your principle · Told: 290 · Believed: 310 · Likely true: 60%*

**In summary.** Every philosophy has some value. What determines that value is the reason you follow it and how it actually helps your life and actions. Philosophies are tools, not teams.

**In depth.**

This principle treats philosophies as tools rather than teams. Instead of asking which philosophy is *right* and joining it, it asks of each: why do you follow it, and how does it actually help your life and actions? On this view, Stoicism, Buddhism, utilitarianism, faith traditions and the rest each carry some value, though not equal value, and part of wisdom is borrowing the right tool for the job.

The Buddha's parable of the raft makes the point. A man builds a raft to cross a dangerous river; on the other side, he doesn't carry the raft on his back forever, however useful it was. Teachings are for crossing, not clinging. William James made a similar argument through pragmatism: judge a belief by the difference it makes in experience. Montaigne, sampling Stoics, Epicureans and skeptics in his *Essays*, modelled the same open, curious approach.

Two questions keep the principle from becoming "anything goes." The first is *why*: a philosophy followed out of fear, tribal loyalty or convenience is held differently from one followed because it genuinely helps. The second is *how much*: some philosophies help a little and harm a lot. That's why the principle needs the book's scale. Every idea in this book sits somewhere above zero, but the distances between them are real, and usefulness must stay checked against evidence and consequences.

The principle may be better named this way than as "value pluralism." Isaiah Berlin's pluralism was about genuine goods conflicting, such as liberty and equality, not about judging philosophies by how they help. The author's version is closer to a practical eclecticism: take what works, notice why, and stay humble.

**Key figures.** The Buddha (c. 5th century BCE) for the raft image; Isaiah Berlin (1909–1997) for value pluralism.

**Key texts.** The Alagaddupama Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 22), containing the parable of the raft; Berlin's lecture "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958).

**Origins and context.** In the raft parable, the Buddha compares his teaching to a raft built to cross a river: useful for crossing, foolish to carry on your back afterward. Berlin, a Latvian-born British philosopher who witnessed the Russian Revolution as a child, argued after World War II that genuine goods conflict and cannot all be ranked on one scale, and that believing otherwise has fueled tyranny.

**Other voices.** William James, who judged beliefs by their practical effects; Montaigne, who sampled many philosophies without committing to one; the Indian Jain doctrine of *anekantavada*, which holds that truth has many sides.

**Related ideas.** The parable of the blind men and the elephant; religious pluralism (John Hick); pragmatism.

**Pros.**
- Encourages humility and curiosity.
- Lets people borrow the best from many traditions.
- Fits the book's scale, where nothing scores below zero.

**Cons.**
- Can slide into "anything goes" if usefulness isn't checked against evidence.
- Some philosophies' value is tiny; the principle needs the scale to stay honest.

**Worth knowing.** The author's own name for this principle may be more accurate than "value pluralism," since Berlin was mainly concerned with competing goods rather than judging philosophies by their helpfulness.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Which philosophy is the right one?
>
> **Author:** I'd ask a different question: what is each one good for, and why do you hold it?
>
> **Ari:** Isn't that a bit relativistic?
>
> **Author:** No. Some philosophies help a lot and some help very little. That's why this book scores them. But almost every one helps someone with something.
>
> **Ari:** Give me an example.
>
> **Author:** Stoicism is great when I'm anxious about what I can't control. Effective altruism is great when I'm deciding where to give. Neither is the whole of life.
>
> **Ari:** And when they conflict?
>
> **Author:** Then I look at why I'm reaching for each one, and what each would actually do in that situation. Tools, not teams.

**Try it.**

- Pick a problem you're facing and ask how two different philosophies from this book would approach it.
- For one belief you hold strongly, write down why you hold it and how it has helped.

## 24. Truth Valued by Consistency, Utility, Simplicity

*Group: Your principle · Told: 290 · Believed: 300 · Likely true: 55%*

**In summary.** Truth is not binary but comes in degrees, like currency. An idea is more valuable the more consistent it is (with itself and with observation), the more useful it is, and the simpler it is. Wrong theories can still pay real dividends: cannons were built on a mistaken theory of projectiles.

**In depth.**

This principle treats truth not as a switch, true or false, but as something with degrees of value, like currency. An idea is worth more the more *consistent* it is (with itself and with what we observe), the more *useful* it is (what it lets us predict, build or live by), and the more *simple* it is (how little it needs to assume). Ideas can be partly right, and partly right ideas can be enormously valuable.

History is full of examples. Early cannon makers used mistaken theories of how projectiles fly and still built working cannons. Newton's physics was overturned by Einstein's, yet it still lands spacecraft. Isaac Asimov's essay "The Relativity of Wrong" made the point with the shape of the Earth: thinking it flat is wrong, thinking it a perfect sphere is also wrong, but much less wrong, and the true slightly squashed shape is better still. Progress is often moving from more wrong to less wrong.

The three criteria echo how scientists choose theories. Thomas Kuhn listed accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness. Ockham's razor favours simpler explanations. George Box summed up the attitude: all models are wrong, but some are useful.

The key safeguard is the meaning of consistency. Pure usefulness could rate a comforting falsehood highly; Bertrand Russell pressed exactly this objection against William James. The author's answer is that consistency includes fit with observation. An idea that clashes with the evidence loses value however comforting it is, so utility can't buy truth on its own.

**Key figures.** Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914) and William James, founders of pragmatism; Niels Bohr (1885–1962).

**Key texts.** Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878); James's *Pragmatism* (1907); Thomas Kuhn's essay "Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice" (1977).

**Origins and context.** Pragmatism arose in post–Civil War America among thinkers impatient with abstract metaphysics. James asked for the "cash value" of an idea: what difference it makes in experience. Bohr, working through the quantum revolution, developed complementarity, the view that apparently contradictory descriptions (wave and particle) can both be needed. A line attributed to him holds that the opposite of a profound truth may be another profound truth.

**Other voices.** William of Ockham (the razor of simplicity); George Box, who wrote in the 1970s that all models are wrong but some are useful; Isaac Asimov, "The Relativity of Wrong" (1989), arguing flat-Earth and round-Earth theories are not equally wrong.

**Related ideas.** Instrumentalism; fuzzy logic; Bayesian degrees of belief; Kuhn's criteria for good theories (accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness).

**Pros.**
- Matches how scientists actually choose between theories.
- Encourages flexibility and openness to better ideas.
- Avoids black-and-white thinking.

**Cons.**
- Without an explicit check against evidence, a comforting falsehood can score well on utility. Bertrand Russell pressed this objection against James.
- Albert Einstein, often grouped with Bohr, was a realist who insisted reality exists independently of our theories; the two debated this for decades.

**Worth knowing.** The author's clarification that "consistency" includes fit with observation answers Russell's objection.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Something is either true or false, isn't it?
>
> **Author:** For simple facts, sure. For theories, it's more like value. Newton was wrong, and we still use him to fly to the Moon.
>
> **Ari:** So how do you judge an idea?
>
> **Author:** Three questions. Does it fit itself and the evidence? Does it help? And how simple is it?
>
> **Ari:** Couldn't a comforting lie score high on "helpful"?
>
> **Author:** That's why consistency includes fit with what we observe. A lie that clashes with the evidence loses value fast.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that make you wishy-washy about truth?
>
> **Author:** The opposite. It makes me ready to trade up whenever a better idea comes along.

**Try it.**

- Take one belief you hold and rate it on consistency, usefulness and simplicity; then look for a rival idea that might score higher.

## 25. Kantian Ethics

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 290 · Believed: 300 · Likely true: 32%*

**In summary.** Act only on principles you could will to become universal laws, and always treat people as ends in themselves, never merely as means. Morality comes from reason and duty, not from feelings or outcomes.

**In depth.**

Kant's ethics rests on the idea that morality must hold for every rational being, regardless of feelings or circumstances. He distinguished *hypothetical* imperatives ("if you want X, do Y") from the *categorical* imperative, which commands unconditionally. He gave several formulations. The first: act only on a maxim, a personal rule, that you could will to be a universal law. If you're tempted to make a false promise to borrow money, imagine everyone doing so; promises would become meaningless and your own plan would collapse. The contradiction shows the act is wrong.

The second formulation is the most influential: treat humanity, whether in yourself or anyone else, always as an end and never merely as a means. You may use a shop assistant's help, but you may not deceive, coerce or manipulate them, because that treats them as a tool rather than a person with their own reason and goals. This idea of inherent human dignity, worth that can't be traded for any price, underpins modern human rights.

For Kant, what makes an action morally good is the will behind it: doing the right thing *because* it's right, from duty, not because it happens to benefit you or because you feel like it. A shopkeeper who gives correct change only to protect his reputation does the right thing, but without moral worth.

The famous problem is the murderer at the door. If a killer asks where your friend is hiding, Kant argued you still may not lie. Most modern Kantians reject his answer. Christine Korsgaard, for instance, argues that deception aimed at someone already acting wrongly against you doesn't violate the spirit of the categorical imperative. The deeper debate is whether moral rules can ever admit exceptions without losing the very universality that makes them moral.

**Key figure.** Immanuel Kant (1724–1804).

**Key texts.** *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785); *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788).

**Origins and context.** Kant lived his whole life in Königsberg, Prussia, at the height of the Enlightenment. He sought a morality grounded in reason alone, universal for all rational beings, at a time when traditional religious authority was being questioned. His ideas also underpin the modern concept of human dignity.

**Other voices.** John Rawls; Christine Korsgaard; Onora O'Neill.

**Related ideas.** Deontology (duty-based ethics); human rights; the Golden Rule.

**Pros.**
- Protects individual dignity; forbids using people.
- Consistent and principled.
- Foundational to human-rights thinking.

**Cons.**
- Rigid: Kant argued it is wrong to lie even to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding.
- Duties can conflict with no clear resolution.
- Downplays emotions and consequences.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** What's wrong with a small lie if it helps someone?
>
> **Kantian:** Try making it a rule for everyone: "lie whenever it seems helpful." Then nobody could trust anything anyone said, and your helpful lie would stop working.
>
> **Ari:** So it's about consistency?
>
> **Kantian:** Partly. And respect. When you deceive someone, you're steering them like an object instead of reasoning with them as a person.
>
> **Ari:** Kant really said you can't lie to a murderer looking for your friend?
>
> **Kantian:** He did, and most of us today think he went wrong there. Lying to someone who's trying to misuse you doesn't disrespect reason the same way.
>
> **Ari:** What do you take from Kant, then?
>
> **Kantian:** That every person has a dignity no amount of usefulness can buy. That's why we say people have rights, not prices.

**Try it.**

- Before acting on a tempting shortcut, ask: what if everyone did this?
- Notice one interaction today where you treated someone merely as a means, such as a server or a caller, and treat them as a person next time.

## 26. Ren and Filial Duty

*Group: Religious message · Told: 290 · Believed: 300 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based tradition)*

**In summary.** *Ren* (humaneness) is the supreme virtue, practiced through proper relationships: ruler and subject, parent and child, elder and younger, friends. Filial piety (*xiao*) is its root.

**In depth.**

*Ren* is usually translated as humaneness or benevolence; the Chinese character combines "person" and "two," suggesting that humanity is something realized between people. When a student asked Confucius what *ren* meant, he answered simply: "Love people." The *Analects* also gives its practical rule: what you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others.

Confucius held that this love must be learned, and that it is learned first at home. Filial piety (*xiao*), respect and care for one's parents, is called the root of *ren*: a child who learns gratitude, respect and responsibility within the family extends them outward to friends, community and state. Society was understood through five relationships: ruler and subject, parent and child, husband and wife, elder and younger sibling, and friend and friend. Each carries mutual obligations. A ruler owes his people benevolence just as they owe him loyalty; a parent owes care just as a child owes respect.

Ritual propriety (*li*) gives these relationships shape: greetings, ceremonies, mourning customs and everyday courtesy. Confucius thought such forms were not empty but trained the heart, the way practising scales trains a musician. The ideal person, the *junzi* or "exemplary person," cultivates virtue through study and self-reflection; Confucius's disciple Zengzi said he examined himself three times a day.

Confucianism shaped East Asian societies for two thousand years, through civil-service examinations based on its classics and through family life. Mencius added an optimistic note: human nature is good, as shown by the instant alarm anyone feels seeing a child about to fall into a well. Critics, ancient and modern, worry that hierarchy can harden into domination and that filial duty can override individual needs, especially those of women and young people.

**Key figure.** Confucius (551–479 BCE).

**Key texts.** The *Analects*; the *Mencius*.

**Origins and context.** Confucius lived during the Spring and Autumn period, as China's Zhou order disintegrated into warring states. He sought to restore social harmony through moral cultivation and ritual. Confucius gave a version of the Golden Rule: do not impose on others what you would not want for yourself.

**Other voices.** Mencius, who argued human nature is good; Xunzi, who argued it must be shaped; Zhu Xi, whose Neo-Confucianism shaped East Asia for centuries.

**Related ideas.** Virtue ethics; care ethics; communitarianism.

**Pros.**
- Strong sense of obligation to others.
- Emphasizes education and self-improvement.
- Supported stable societies for millennia.

**Cons.**
- Can entrench rigid hierarchies.
- Filial duty may override individual needs.
- Historically subordinated women.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Why does Confucius care so much about how I treat my parents?
>
> **Confucian:** Because home is where you first learn to care for others. Gratitude, respect and patience learned there spread outward.
>
> **Ari:** What if my parents don't deserve respect?
>
> **Confucian:** The relationship has duties both ways. A parent owes care too. Confucius even said a child may gently disagree with a parent who's in the wrong.
>
> **Ari:** All those rituals seem old-fashioned.
>
> **Confucian:** Think of them as habits of respect. Saying thank you, greeting elders, marking important days. Small forms train the heart.
>
> **Ari:** And the goal of it all?
>
> **Confucian:** To become a person of *ren*, humaneness. Confucius summed it up in two words: love people.

**Try it.**

- Call or visit a parent, grandparent or elder this week, and thank them for something specific.
- Try Zengzi's daily self-examination: was I loyal to others today, trustworthy with friends, and did I practise what I learned?

## 27. Conservatism

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 290 · Believed: 300 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Human nature is limited, and inherited institutions (family, religion, community, law) carry hard-won wisdom. Government should preserve what works and change carefully.

**In depth.**

Edmund Burke's central idea is that society is not a machine to be redesigned but something more like a living inheritance. He described it as a partnership "between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born." Customs, institutions and moral intuitions have been tested by generations of experience, and they often contain wisdom nobody can fully articulate. A reformer who tears them down because he can't see their purpose may destroy something he didn't understand.

G. K. Chesterton made the same point with his fence. A reformer finds a fence across a road and says, "I don't see the use of this; let's clear it away." The wiser reformer answers: "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go and find out why it was built. Then we'll talk." Conservatism, in this sense, is not opposition to change but caution about it: change gradually, test as you go, and preserve what works.

Thomas Sowell's "constrained vision" captures conservatism's view of human nature. People are limited in knowledge and virtue, and can't be perfected by better institutions. So the goal is not to design an ideal society but to manage trade-offs, using institutions like families, churches, local communities, markets and the rule of law that channel imperfect people toward decent outcomes. Michael Oakeshott added a temperament: to prefer the familiar to the unknown, the tried to the untried, present laughter to utopian bliss.

Conservatism has many strands: religious, traditionalist, free-market, national. They sometimes conflict, for instance when markets disrupt the communities traditionalists want to preserve. Its critics point out that "what works" has often worked for some people at others' expense, and that caution has at times delayed reforms like the abolition of slavery and women's suffrage.

**Key figure.** Edmund Burke (1729–1797).

**Key text.** *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790).

**Origins and context.** Burke, an Irish-born British statesman, predicted that the French Revolution's attempt to rebuild society from abstract principles would end in tyranny. The Reign of Terror (1793–94) seemed to vindicate him.

**Other voices.** Michael Oakeshott, "On Being Conservative" (1956); Russell Kirk, *The Conservative Mind* (1953); Roger Scruton; G. K. Chesterton's "fence" principle (don't remove a fence until you know why it was built).

**Related ideas.** Tradition; subsidiarity; Sowell's constrained vision.

**Pros.**

- Values community and continuity.

**Cons.**
- Can defend unfairness that could be fixed.
- Resistance to change has at times opposed reforms later widely accepted.

## 28. Progressive Liberalism

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 290 · Believed: 300 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Government should actively correct unfair outcomes and protect the vulnerable.

**In depth.**

Progressive liberalism keeps classical liberalism's commitment to individual freedom but argues that freedom needs more than the absence of interference. A child born into poverty, without decent schooling or healthcare, is not meaningfully free to pursue her goals, even if no law stops her. British "new liberals" like T. H. Green called this *positive freedom*: the real ability to act on your choices. Government, in this view, should actively remove obstacles: provide education, regulate dangerous workplaces, break up monopolies, protect civil rights, and support people through hardship.

John Rawls gave the view its most famous philosophical form. Imagine designing society's rules from behind a "veil of ignorance," not knowing whether you'll be rich or poor, healthy or disabled, of which race, sex or talent. Rawls argued that rational people would choose two principles: equal basic liberties for all, and inequalities allowed only if they improve the position of the least advantaged (the "difference principle") and come with fair equality of opportunity.

Historically, progressivism has driven many reforms now accepted across politics: child-labour laws, the eight-hour day, food-safety rules, Social Security, the civil-rights acts of the 1960s. Sowell's "unconstrained vision" describes its outlook: human problems are largely caused by bad institutions, and better ones can solve them.

Critics, including conservatives and libertarians, argue that governments often lack the knowledge to fix complex problems and that programmes can have unintended effects. The book raises a specific concern: when progressive arguments are framed as collective guilt, telling some groups they are responsible for others' suffering, they can provoke resentment and backlash rather than compassion. The same goals framed as shared improvement often win wider support.

**Key figure.** John Rawls (1921–2002).

**Key text.** *A Theory of Justice* (1971).

**Origins and context.** Progressivism arose in the Gilded Age (1890s–1920s) against child labor, monopolies, and corruption, and grew through the New Deal (1933) and civil-rights era. Rawls asked what rules you would choose behind a "veil of ignorance," not knowing your place in society.

**Other voices.** T. H. Green and L. T. Hobhouse (British "new liberals"); John Dewey; Franklin D. Roosevelt.

**Related ideas.** Civil rights; regulation; Sowell's unconstrained vision.

**Pros.**
- Builds compassion for the disadvantaged.
- Drove reforms like labor protections and civil rights.

**Cons.**
- When framed as collective guilt, it can trigger the resentment the author warns about.
- Critics argue it can expand government beyond its competence.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If everyone has equal rights, why does government need to do more?
>
> **Progressive:** Because rights on paper aren't the same as real chances. A kid in a failing school has the same rights as a rich kid, but not the same options.
>
> **Ari:** So the government should make everyone equal?
>
> **Progressive:** Not equal outcomes. Fair chances, and a floor nobody falls below.
>
> **Ari:** How do you decide what's fair?
>
> **Progressive:** Rawls had a test: design the rules without knowing which life you'll be born into. Most people choose a safety net and good public schools.
>
> **Ari:** Some people hear all this and feel blamed.
>
> **Progressive:** That's a fair warning. It works better as "let's build a fairer road for everyone" than as "you owe for what others suffered."

## 29. Radical Acceptance

*Group: Strategy · Told: 280 · Believed: 300 · Likely true: not assessed (a strategy)*

**In summary.** Fully accept reality as it is, including pain you cannot change, without approving of it. Fighting what already is turns pain into suffering; acceptance frees energy for what can be changed.

**In depth.**

Radical acceptance means fully acknowledging reality as it is, with mind, body and heart, especially the parts you cannot change. It does not mean approving of what happened, liking it, giving up, or pretending it doesn't hurt. Marsha Linehan distinguishes *pain*, which is part of life, from *suffering*, which she describes as pain plus non-acceptance: the exhausting, repeated fight against the fact that something has already happened. "This shouldn't be happening" is the thought that keeps pain spinning.

Linehan developed the skill within Dialectical Behavior Therapy, which she created for people with intense emotional distress and suicidal behaviour. The central "dialectic" is between acceptance and change: patients learn to accept themselves and their situation completely *and* to work hard at changing what they can. Each half makes the other possible. People who believe they must reject themselves before improving often stay stuck; acceptance lowers the emotional temperature enough to act wisely.

The practice is concrete. Notice when you are fighting reality ("it's not fair," "this can't be"). Remind yourself that the reality is what it is, and that it has causes, even if you don't like them. Practise accepting with your body too: relaxed hands, a half-smile, slow breathing. Expect to repeat it; acceptance often has to be chosen many times a day, what Linehan calls "turning the mind."

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, developed by Steven Hayes, makes a similar move: stop wrestling with painful thoughts and feelings, and commit to actions guided by your values instead. Both have good research support. The risk is misusing acceptance to tolerate what should be changed. The Serenity Prayer's balance captures the answer: accept what you cannot change, change what you can, and learn to tell the difference.

**Key figure.** Marsha Linehan (born 1943), psychologist.

**Key texts.** Linehan's *Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder* (1993); Tara Brach's *Radical Acceptance* (2003).

**Origins and context.** Linehan developed Dialectical Behavior Therapy in the 1980s and 1990s for people with severe emotional distress and suicidal behavior, drawing on Zen practice. She later revealed she had struggled with these conditions herself. DBT balances acceptance with change, a "dialectic."

**Other voices.** Steven Hayes, founder of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy; Reinhold Niebuhr's Serenity Prayer (1930s); Epictetus; the Buddha.

**Related ideas.** Mindfulness; Stoicism; the author's relative deprivation principle.

**Pros.**
- Proven component of effective therapy.
- Reduces needless suffering.
- Distinguishes acceptance from approval.

**Cons.**
- Can slide into passivity toward fixable harm.
- Hard to practice in fresh grief or trauma.
- Misunderstood as "giving up."

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** My partner left me and I keep thinking it shouldn't have happened.
>
> **Therapist:** It did happen. Fighting that fact is a big part of why you're exhausted.
>
> **Ari:** So I'm supposed to be fine with it?
>
> **Therapist:** No. Accepting isn't approving. It hurts, and you can let it hurt. Just stop arguing with the fact that it's real.
>
> **Ari:** How is that different from giving up?
>
> **Therapist:** Giving up is "nothing can change." Acceptance is "this is where I am." Only from where you actually are can you start moving.
>
> **Ari:** I accept it, and an hour later I'm angry again.
>
> **Therapist:** That's normal. Turn your mind back, gently, as many times as you need. That's the practice.

**Try it.**

- When you notice "this shouldn't be happening," replace it with "this is happening, and it has causes."
- Practise "willing hands": rest your hands open, palms up, and take three slow breaths while thinking about something you can't change.
- Write two lists: what in this situation I can change, and what I need to accept.

## 30. Unconditional Grace

*Group: Religious message · Told: 280 · Believed: 290 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Salvation and love are given freely by God, not earned by good deeds.

**In depth.**

The Christian teaching of grace holds that salvation is a gift. Paul writes to the Ephesians: "For it is by grace you have been saved, through faith, and this is not from yourselves, it is the gift of God, not by works, so that no one can boast." Nothing a person does can earn God's love; it is given freely, even to the worst sinner who accepts it. In Luke's Gospel, the parable of the prodigal son shows the same thing in a family: a son wastes his inheritance, returns in disgrace, and his father runs to embrace him before he can finish apologizing.

Martin Luther experienced this as liberation. As a monk he tormented himself trying to be good enough for God, confessing for hours. His discovery that righteousness comes through faith, not achievement, freed him from that scrupulosity, and his protest against selling indulgences, payments to reduce punishment for sins, launched the Reformation.

The danger was recognized early. If grace is free, why not sin freely? Paul himself answered: "Shall we go on sinning so that grace may increase? By no means!" Grace is meant to transform, not excuse. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing in Nazi Germany, named the corruption "cheap grace": forgiveness without repentance, comfort without discipleship. He contrasted it with "costly grace," which calls a person to follow Christ even at great cost. Bonhoeffer was executed for resisting the Nazi regime in 1945.

Psychologically, unconditional acceptance resembles Carl Rogers's "unconditional positive regard," which he found essential for growth in therapy: people change more readily when they feel accepted than when they feel judged. The book's caution comes from studies by Azim Shariff and others suggesting that thinking about a forgiving God, without accountability, can slightly loosen people's behaviour. Grace seems to do the most good when paired with a call to change.

**Key figures.** The Apostle Paul (c. 5–c. 64 CE); Martin Luther (1483–1546).

**Key texts.** Paul's letters (Romans; Ephesians 2:8–9); Luther's Ninety-Five Theses (1517).

**Origins and context.** Paul preached grace to early Christian communities debating whether non-Jews must follow Jewish law. Luther revived the idea against the sale of indulgences, sparking the Protestant Reformation.

**Other voices.** Augustine; John Newton, who wrote "Amazing Grace" (1772) after leaving the slave trade; Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who warned against "cheap grace" in *The Cost of Discipleship* (1937); Shinran, whose Pure Land Buddhism taught a parallel reliance on Amida Buddha's grace.

**Related ideas.** Self-compassion; unconditional positive regard (Carl Rogers).

**Pros.**
- Relieves shame and perfectionism.
- Affirms worth independent of performance.

**Cons.**
- Azim Shariff's lab studies found people who reflected on divine forgiveness cheated more than those who reflected on punishment.
- Can be taken as license, which is what Bonhoeffer warned against.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If God forgives everything, why bother being good?
>
> **Pastor:** That's the oldest question about grace. Paul's answer was "by no means." You don't stay in the mud because someone pulled you out.
>
> **Ari:** Then what's grace for?
>
> **Pastor:** To free you from trying to earn love. People who feel accepted change more than people who feel condemned.
>
> **Ari:** Some people use it as a free pass.
>
> **Pastor:** Bonhoeffer called that cheap grace: forgiveness with no change. Real grace costs something, because it turns your life around.
>
> **Ari:** And if I can't believe I'm forgivable?
>
> **Pastor:** That's exactly who grace is for. The prodigal son didn't even finish his apology before his father hugged him.

**Try it.**

- Write down one thing you've been trying to "earn" forgiveness for, and practise accepting that you can start again today.
- Offer someone forgiveness this week without requiring them to earn it first, while still being honest about what happened.

## 31. Moral Objectivity

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 270 · Believed: 280 · Likely true: 62%*

**In summary.** Some moral truths hold regardless of what anyone thinks or feels, the way mathematical truths do. Torturing innocents for fun is wrong, whether or not a culture approves.

**In depth.**

Moral realism holds that some moral claims are true or false regardless of anyone's opinion, much like "2 + 2 = 4" or "the Earth orbits the Sun." When we say that torturing an innocent child for fun is wrong, realists say we are stating a fact, not expressing a feeling or a cultural habit. A society that approved of it would simply be mistaken, the way a society that believed the Earth was flat was mistaken.

Arguments for realism often start from how morality actually feels to us. Moral disagreements look like arguments about facts: we give reasons, change our minds, and speak of moral progress. Most people believe the abolition of slavery was genuine moral improvement, not just a change of taste. If morality were merely preference, "progress" would make no sense. Derek Parfit spent the last decades of his life defending a non-naturalist realism, arguing that there are irreducibly normative truths about what we have reason to do, and that the major ethical theories, properly understood, are "climbing the same mountain on different sides."

The puzzles are real too. If moral facts exist, what are they made of, and how do we detect them? J. L. Mackie called them "queer": unlike anything else in the universe, they would somehow be objectively action-guiding. Naturalist realists answer that moral facts are facts about well-being and flourishing, which science can partly study. Non-naturalists answer that we grasp them by reason, the way we grasp mathematical truths.

The 2020 PhilPapers survey found about 62% of professional philosophers accept or lean toward moral realism, a majority but far from unanimous. In this book's scale, perfect moral objectivity sits at the top, 500 sparkles: the idealized point where one would know and do what is truly best.

**Key figures.** Plato (c. 428–348 BCE); in modern times, Derek Parfit (1942–2017).

**Key texts.** Plato's *Republic*; G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903); Parfit's *On What Matters* (2011); Russ Shafer-Landau's *Moral Realism: A Defence* (2003).

**Origins and context.** Plato argued that "the Good" exists as an eternal Form, discoverable by reason. The view dominated Western thought for centuries, was challenged by Hume and twentieth-century positivists, and revived in late twentieth-century analytic philosophy.

**Other voices.** Thomas Aquinas (natural law); Thomas Nagel; David Enoch.

**Related ideas.** Natural law; moral realism; universal human rights.

**Pros.**
- Gives firm grounds to condemn atrocities.
- A small 2013 study (Young and Durwin) found people prompted toward realism donated more.
- About 62% of philosophers accepted or leaned toward moral realism in 2020.

**Cons.**
- Hard to explain where moral facts come from or how we know them.
- Can breed rigidity or self-righteousness.
- Cultures disagree deeply on moral questions.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Isn't morality just what each culture decides?
>
> **Realist:** Was slavery wrong even when most cultures accepted it?
>
> **Ari:** Of course.
>
> **Realist:** Then you're a realist about at least one moral claim. You think it was wrong regardless of what people believed then.
>
> **Ari:** But where would moral facts even be? I can't see them.
>
> **Realist:** You can't see numbers either, but "2 + 2 = 4" is still true. Some of us think moral truths are known by reason; others think they're facts about what helps or harms living beings.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that make people self-righteous?
>
> **Realist:** It can. Believing there's a truth isn't the same as believing you have it. Realists should be the humblest people around, since they can be wrong.

**Try it.**

- Think of one moral belief you'd hold even if everyone around you disagreed, and write down why.
- Think of one moral belief you've changed your mind about, and what convinced you.

## 32. Hell and Divine Punishment

*Group: Religious message · Told: 270 · Believed: 260 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Wrongdoing will be punished after death.

**In depth.**

The idea that wrongdoing will be judged after death developed gradually. Early Hebrew scripture describes *Sheol*, a shadowy place where all the dead go, good and bad alike. Ideas of final judgment, reward and punishment grew stronger in later Jewish writings, likely influenced by Persian Zoroastrianism, which taught a judgment of souls and a final renewal of the world. By the time of Jesus, *Gehenna*, named after a valley outside Jerusalem associated with burning and earlier child sacrifice, had become an image of punishment for the wicked.

In Christianity and Islam, hell took vivid forms. Matthew 25 describes the Son of Man separating sheep from goats, sending those who failed to feed the hungry or visit the sick "into the eternal fire." The Quran describes Jahannam in many passages, alongside repeated emphasis on God's mercy. Medieval Europe gave hell its most detailed imagery, culminating in Dante's *Inferno*, where punishments fit each sin. Hindu and Buddhist traditions also describe hells, but usually as temporary stages within the cycle of rebirth rather than eternal destinations.

Within Christianity there has always been debate. Some theologians, from Origen in the third century to David Bentley Hart today, argue for universal salvation: that a loving God will ultimately restore everyone. Others hold to eternal conscious punishment, or to annihilation of the wicked. Many modern believers describe hell less as a torture chamber than as the self-chosen state of separation from God.

The book scores this message lower when *believed* than when *told*, and the reason is psychological. As a background idea, accountability after death may discourage some wrongdoing. But taken fully to heart, especially by children or anxious people, eternal punishment can produce intense fear, scrupulosity and religious trauma, and it motivates good behaviour through dread rather than care.

**Key figures.** Jesus (in the Gospels' warnings); the Prophet Muhammad.

**Key texts.** Matthew 25; the Quran's descriptions of Jahannam; Dante's *Inferno* (c. 1308–1320).

**Origins and context.** Early Hebrew scripture spoke of Sheol, a shadowy abode of the dead; ideas of judgment developed later, influenced by Persian thought. Hell became vivid in medieval Christianity and Islam. Hindu and Buddhist traditions also describe hells, usually temporary.

**Other voices.** Jonathan Edwards's sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741); Blaise Pascal's wager; universalists from Origen to David Bentley Hart (*That All Shall Be Saved*, 2019).

**Related ideas.** Supernatural punishment hypothesis; deterrence theory.

**Pros.**
- Shariff and Rigdon (2012) found national belief in hell predicted lower crime, though critics questioned the analysis.
- Reinforces moral seriousness.

**Cons.**
- Fear-based belief can produce anxiety and scrupulosity, especially in children.
- Motivation through fear rather than care.
- Full belief deepens the fear, which is why "believed" scores lower than "told."

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Does believing in hell actually make people behave better?
>
> **Researcher:** Some studies suggest a modest link. Countries with stronger belief in hell had slightly lower crime rates in one analysis, though that's been questioned.
>
> **Ari:** So it works?
>
> **Researcher:** As a deterrent, perhaps a little. But fear is a costly motivator. Some people, especially kids, become terrified of tiny mistakes.
>
> **Ari:** Do all religions teach eternal hell?
>
> **Researcher:** No. Many Hindus and Buddhists see hells as temporary. And plenty of Christians, past and present, have believed God will eventually save everyone.
>
> **Ari:** What's the better motivation, then?
>
> **Researcher:** Most evidence points to care, connection and a sense that your actions matter now. Fear can stop harm in the moment; love sustains goodness over a lifetime.

**Try it.**

- Notice one thing you do mainly out of fear of punishment, and ask what reason you'd have to do it out of care.

## 33. Karma

*Group: Religious message · Told: 270 · Believed: 270 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Actions have consequences that shape future experiences, in this life or the next.

**In depth.**

*Karma* means "action" in Sanskrit. The teaching holds that actions, along with the intentions behind them, leave traces that shape future experience: in this life, and in many traditions, future lives through rebirth (*samsara*). Good actions tend to bring good results, harmful actions harmful ones, not as rewards handed out by a judge but as natural consequences, like seeds producing their own kind of fruit.

Traditions understand it differently. In many Hindu schools karma works within the cycle of rebirth, and liberation (*moksha*) means escaping the cycle altogether. The Bhagavad Gita adds a crucial refinement, *karma yoga*: act according to your duty, but without attachment to the fruits of your action. "You have a right to your actions, but never to their fruits." This lets a person work hard and well while releasing anxiety about outcomes. The Buddha emphasized intention (*cetana*) as the heart of karma, and taught that karma is not fate: present choices can change future direction. Jainism treats karma almost physically, as fine matter that clings to the soul through harmful acts and must be shed through discipline.

The concept spread worldwide, often simplified into "what goes around comes around." That popular version can do real good, encouraging long-term thinking and reminding people that cruelty tends to rebound.

It also carries a well-studied risk. Psychologist Melvin Lerner found that people strongly want to believe the world is just, and when they see an innocent victim they often blame the victim rather than accept that suffering can be random. A belief that misfortune reflects past deeds can feed that tendency, and historically karma was used to justify caste: people born into low status were said to deserve it. Many teachers today stress that karma explains, at most, one's *own* path, and never licenses judging someone else's suffering.

**Key figures.** The sages of the Upanishads; Krishna in the *Bhagavad Gita*.

**Key texts.** The *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* (c. 700 BCE); the *Bhagavad Gita*.

**Origins and context.** Karma developed in Vedic India and became central to Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. The *Gita* teaches acting without attachment to results, close to the author's principle on expectations. Buddhism emphasized intention as the core of karma.

**Other voices.** The Buddha; Swami Vivekananda; Melvin Lerner, whose "just-world hypothesis" research (1960s–80s) showed people tend to blame victims to preserve belief in a fair world.

**Related ideas.** Cause and effect; the just-world hypothesis; "what goes around comes around."

**Pros.**
- Motivates long-term ethical behavior.
- No single failure is final.

**Cons.**
- Can lead people to blame victims for their misfortune.
- Historically used to justify caste hierarchy.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** So if something bad happens to me, I deserved it?
>
> **Teacher:** That's the misunderstanding that does the most harm. Karma is about how your actions shape your path, not a scoreboard for judging anyone's pain.
>
> **Ari:** Then what's the point of it?
>
> **Teacher:** It reminds you that your choices ripple outward, and that cruelty tends to come back around, inside you if nowhere else.
>
> **Ari:** The Gita says not to care about results?
>
> **Teacher:** Not to be *attached* to them. Do your work well, then let go of the outcome. You'll work better and worry less.
>
> **Ari:** And if I've done bad things?
>
> **Teacher:** Karma isn't fate. The next action is still yours. Every good act plants a different seed.

**Try it.**

- Do one piece of work today as well as you can, then deliberately let go of how it's received.
- When you see someone suffering, catch any thought that they "deserved it," and replace it with "how can I help?"

## 34. Libertarianism

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 270 · Believed: 270 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Government's only legitimate job is protecting individual liberty from force and fraud.

**In depth.**

Libertarians hold that individuals own themselves: your body, your labour and what you acquire through fair means are yours. The only legitimate use of force is to protect people from force and fraud. This yields the *non-aggression principle*: no one may initiate violence or coercion against another person or their property. Taxation beyond what's needed for protection, laws against victimless choices, and most regulation are, in this view, forms of coercion, however well-meant.

Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* made the philosophical case. He argued that a minimal "night-watchman" state, limited to protection, courts and enforcing contracts, can be justified, but nothing more. His famous Wilt Chamberlain example challenges redistribution: start from any distribution you consider fair. Now suppose a million fans each freely pay a basketball star 25 cents to watch him play. He ends up rich, the distribution is now unequal, yet every step was voluntary. To restore the original pattern, Nozick argued, you'd have to keep interfering with people's free choices.

Libertarianism takes many forms. Minarchists accept a small state; anarcho-capitalists reject the state entirely in favour of private protection and courts. Left-libertarians accept self-ownership but argue that natural resources like land belong to everyone, justifying taxes on them. Consistent libertarians also oppose government on social issues: drug prohibition, censorship, surveillance and military adventurism.

Critics argue that libertarianism underrates how unequal bargaining power makes some "voluntary" choices hollow (a desperate worker may accept almost anything), how markets fail with public goods and pollution, and how much everyone's wealth depends on shared infrastructure and institutions. Libertarians answer that governments fail too, often worse, and that freedom is a moral good in its own right, not just a means to prosperity.

**Key figure.** Robert Nozick (1938–2002).

**Key texts.** Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974); Murray Rothbard's *For a New Liberty* (1973).

**Origins and context.** Modern libertarianism grew in 1970s America in reaction to expanding government. Nozick wrote partly in response to his Harvard colleague Rawls.

**Other voices.** Ayn Rand; Milton Friedman; left-libertarians who pair self-ownership with shared natural resources.

**Related ideas.** The non-aggression principle; classical liberalism; anarcho-capitalism.

**Pros.**
- Strong defense of personal freedom.
- Encourages self-responsibility.

**Cons.**
- Weaker sense of obligation to others.
- Critics argue it underestimates market failures and unequal bargaining power.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Libertarians just don't want to pay taxes, right?
>
> **Libertarian:** It's deeper than that. I think forcing people to do things, outside of protecting others, needs a very strong justification. Most of what governments do doesn't have one.
>
> **Ari:** Even schools and roads?
>
> **Libertarian:** Some of us accept a small state for things like that. The question is always: is force really necessary, or could people cooperate voluntarily?
>
> **Ari:** What about people who are poor through no fault of their own?
>
> **Libertarian:** I want them helped, voluntarily: through charity, mutual aid and growing prosperity. Compulsory help still means taking from someone by force.
>
> **Ari:** And drugs, or what adults do privately?
>
> **Libertarian:** Your body, your choice. That cuts across left and right, which is why we annoy both.

**Try it.**

- For one law you support, ask what it would mean to enforce it by force against someone who refuses, and whether you'd still support it.

## 35. Former Belief: The Law of Conservation of Emotion

*Group: Your former belief · Told: 260 · Believed: 240 · Likely true: 25%*

**In summary.** Every good experience is eventually balanced by a bad one, and vice versa. Exercise hurts now and feels good later; drugs feel good now and hurt later. So it's best to do the suffering first.

**In depth.**

This was the author's earlier view: every good experience is eventually balanced by a bad one, and every bad one by a good one, like a law of conservation in physics. Its practical rule was to do the suffering first, such as exercise, study and discipline, so that the good comes later, rather than taking pleasure now and paying afterwards.

It had real support. Richard Solomon's opponent-process theory found that strong emotional reactions are followed by opposite after-reactions: the fear of a first skydive gives way to elation, and a drug's high gives way to a crash. With repetition the first reaction weakens and the after-reaction strengthens, which explains both addiction (diminishing highs, deepening lows) and why exercise becomes rewarding over time. Anna Lembke's *Dopamine Nation* describes a similar pleasure-pain see-saw in the brain's reward system.

The belief did a lot of good. It built discipline, made hardship feel purposeful, and protected against short-term traps. That's why it scores reasonably high when told.

The author moved beyond it for three reasons. First, emotion isn't strictly conserved: kindness, gratitude and relationships seem to leave a net positive, and trauma can leave a lasting net negative. Second, believed fully, it can make a person distrust happiness, always bracing for the payback, which is why its score drops when believed. Third, it contradicts the later principle that good can accumulate without limit. What survives is a narrower, truer lesson: short-term pleasure often costs long-term good.

**Key figure.** Richard Solomon (1925–1995), an American psychologist.

**Key text.** Solomon and John Corbit, "An Opponent-Process Theory of Motivation" (1974), *Psychological Review*.

**Origins and context.** Solomon studied how emotions rebound: the terror of a first skydive followed by elation, the high of a drug followed by a crash. He found that with repetition the initial reaction weakens and the after-reaction strengthens, explaining both addiction and why exercise becomes rewarding. The research came during a period of intense interest in addiction and motivation.

**Other voices.** The Stoics, who practiced voluntary discomfort; Walter Mischel's delayed-gratification research (whose later replications found smaller effects than first claimed); Anna Lembke's *Dopamine Nation* (2021), describing a pleasure-pain balance in the brain.

**Related ideas.** Hedonic adaptation; the pleasure-pain see-saw; "no pain, no gain."

**Pros.**
- Builds discipline and resistance to short-term traps.
- Helps people endure worthwhile hardship.

**Cons.**
- Not strictly conserved: kindness, gratitude, and relationships appear to leave a net positive; trauma can leave a lasting net negative.
- Full belief can make people distrust happiness, always bracing for payback.
- Contradicts the author's later view that good can accumulate without limit.

**Worth knowing.** Reframed for the current system: *short-term pleasure often costs long-term good.*

**In conversation.**

## 36. Existentialism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 260 · Believed: 270 · Likely true: 40%*

**In summary.** Existence precedes essence: humans are not born with a fixed purpose but create meaning through choices. We are radically free and fully responsible, and pretending otherwise is "bad faith."

**In depth.**

Sartre's slogan "existence precedes essence" is easiest to grasp with his own example of a paper-knife. Its maker had a purpose in mind before making it, so its essence, what it is for, came first. Humans, Sartre argued, have no such maker or blueprint. We exist first, find ourselves in the world, and then define ourselves through our choices. There is no fixed human nature that tells you who to be.

This means radical freedom and total responsibility. Even refusing to choose is a choice. Sartre called the attempt to escape this freedom *bad faith*: pretending we have no choice ("I had to; it's my job"; "that's just who I am"). His famous example is a café waiter who plays the role of waiter a little too perfectly, as if he were a waiter the way a table is a table, denying that he could walk away at any moment. The freedom can feel dizzying; Sartre described the resulting *anguish* as the price of being human.

Simone de Beauvoir developed the ethics. In *The Ethics of Ambiguity* she argued that genuinely willing your own freedom means willing others' freedom too, since meaning is created together. In *The Second Sex* she applied existentialism to women: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman," showing how social roles are presented as natural facts to limit choices.

Existentialism's roots go back to Kierkegaard, who stressed the individual's leap of faith, and Nietzsche, who called people to create their own values. Viktor Frankl, who survived Auschwitz, drew a practical psychology from it: even in the worst conditions, a person retains the freedom to choose their attitude, and finding meaning is what sustains life. The book's caution is that existentialism can underplay how much circumstances shape choice, the opposite emphasis from the author's motivations principle.

**Key figure.** Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980).

**Key texts.** Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) and *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946); Simone de Beauvoir's *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947).

**Origins and context.** Existentialism flowered in Nazi-occupied and postwar France, when traditional certainties had collapsed and individuals faced stark choices between collaboration and resistance. Its roots go back to Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) and Friedrich Nietzsche.

**Other voices.** Kierkegaard; Nietzsche; Martin Heidegger; Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946); Dostoevsky.

**Related ideas.** Absurdism; humanistic psychology; authenticity.

**Pros.**
- Empowers people to take responsibility.
- Encourages authenticity.
- Inspired psychotherapy approaches like logotherapy.

**Cons.**
- Radical freedom can feel overwhelming.
- Underplays how circumstances constrain choice, the opposite of the author's motivations principle.
- Offers little concrete moral guidance.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** What's the meaning of life, according to existentialists?
>
> **Existentialist:** There isn't one handed to you. You make it, by how you live.
>
> **Ari:** That's a lot of pressure.
>
> **Existentialist:** It is. Sartre called it anguish. But it also means nobody can tell you who you must be.
>
> **Ari:** I don't really have choices, though. I need my job.
>
> **Existentialist:** You've chosen to keep it, for good reasons. Saying "I have no choice" is what Sartre called bad faith. Owning the choice is the first step to changing it if you want to.
>
> **Ari:** And other people?
>
> **Existentialist:** De Beauvoir's point: you can't truly want freedom for yourself without wanting it for others. We make meaning together.

**Try it.**

- Catch yourself saying "I have to" today and rephrase it as "I choose to, because…"
- Write down one way you'd define yourself that you chose, not one you were given.

## 37. Epicureanism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 260 · Believed: 270 · Likely true: 35%*

**In summary.** The goal of life is pleasure, understood as tranquility (*ataraxia*) and freedom from pain, not indulgence. Simple food, friendship, and freedom from fear of death and the gods bring the deepest happiness.

**In depth.**

Epicurus taught that pleasure is the beginning and end of the good life, a claim that shocked his contemporaries and still gets misunderstood. By pleasure he meant mainly the *absence* of pain: *aponia*, no bodily pain, and *ataraxia*, no mental disturbance. A simple meal when hungry, conversation with friends, and a mind free from fear were, for him, the highest pleasures. Luxuries tend to bring new anxieties and dependencies.

He sorted desires into three kinds. *Natural and necessary* desires, like food, shelter and friendship, are easy to satisfy and should be. *Natural but unnecessary* ones, like gourmet food, are fine occasionally but not worth chasing. *Empty* desires, like fame, power and endless wealth, have no natural limit and can never be satisfied, so they bring only restlessness. "Nothing is enough for the man to whom enough is too little."

Friendship held a special place: "Of all the means which wisdom acquires to ensure happiness throughout the whole of life, by far the most important is friendship." His school, the Garden, was a community of friends living simply together, unusually open to women and enslaved people.

Epicurus also targeted the two great fears. Fear of the gods was groundless, he argued, because the gods, if they exist, don't intervene in human affairs. Fear of death is irrational because "death is nothing to us": where death is, we are not, and where we are, death is not. He summed up his teaching in a fourfold remedy (*tetrapharmakos*): don't fear the gods; don't fear death; what is good is easy to get; what is terrible is easy to endure. Modern happiness research, which finds that relationships and modest security matter far more than status or extra wealth, often sounds strikingly Epicurean.

**Key figure.** Epicurus (341–270 BCE).

**Key texts.** Epicurus's *Letter to Menoeceus* and *Principal Doctrines*; Lucretius's poem *On the Nature of Things* (c. 50s BCE).

**Origins and context.** Epicurus founded his school, the Garden, in Athens around 307 BCE, welcoming women and slaves. Like the Stoics, he sought peace in a turbulent post-Alexander world. Lucretius's poem was rediscovered in 1417, influencing the Renaissance and early science (told in Stephen Greenblatt's *The Swerve*, 2011).

**Other voices.** Lucretius; Thomas Jefferson, who called himself an Epicurean; Michel de Montaigne.

**Related ideas.** Hedonism; minimalism; atomism; utilitarianism.

**Pros.**
- Values friendship and simplicity.
- Eases fear of death.
- Fits research linking relationships to happiness.

**Cons.**
- Withdrawal from public life can neglect civic duty.
- Pleasure-based ethics can be misread as indulgence.
- Its atomism-based worldview offers no larger purpose.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Epicureans were all about pleasure? Sounds like a party.
>
> **Epicurean:** Our parties were bread, water, a little cheese and good friends. Epicurus thought simple pleasures are the deepest ones.
>
> **Ari:** Why not chase bigger pleasures?
>
> **Epicurean:** Fame, luxury, endless money: they never satisfy, and they bring worry with them. Enough is a feast.
>
> **Ari:** What about fear of death?
>
> **Epicurean:** Where death is, you are not. You'll never experience being dead, so there's nothing in it to suffer.
>
> **Ari:** And what's the most important thing?
>
> **Epicurean:** Friends. Of everything wisdom gives for a happy life, Epicurus said friendship is by far the greatest.

**Try it.**

## 38. Wu Wei

*Group: Religious message · Told: 260 · Believed: 270 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based tradition)*

**In summary.** "Effortless action": act in harmony with the natural flow of things, without forcing.

**In depth.**

*Wu wei* is often translated as "non-action," but it means something closer to effortless or unforced action: acting in harmony with the way things naturally move, without strain, struggle or ego. The *Tao Te Ching* uses water as its central image. Water is the softest thing, yet nothing is better at wearing down the hard; it flows around obstacles, settles in low places others avoid, and nourishes everything without competing.

Zhuangzi gave the idea its most memorable stories. Cook Ding carves an ox so skilfully that his knife never dulls in nineteen years: he no longer sees the whole ox but follows its natural joints, letting the blade pass through the spaces. A swimmer survives a waterfall's churning pool by going down with the whirl and up with the swirl, following the water's way rather than his own. In both, mastery looks effortless because the person has stopped forcing.

For rulers, Laozi taught governing lightly: "Govern a large state as you would cook a small fish," without too much stirring, or it falls apart. The best leaders are barely noticed; when the work is done, people say, "We did it ourselves." Taoism developed partly as a critique of Confucian ritual and Legalist control, both of which, it suggested, imposed artificial order.

Edward Slingerland points out a paradox in *Trying Not to Try*: you can't achieve spontaneity by trying hard to be spontaneous. Modern research on flow, the absorbed state Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described, suggests the answer lies in practice. Skills practised deeply enough become effortless, and challenges matched to skill let attention flow. Wu wei, then, is often the reward of long effort, not a substitute for it.

**Key figures.** Laozi (traditionally 6th century BCE); Zhuangzi (c. 369–286 BCE).

**Key texts.** The *Tao Te Ching*; the *Zhuangzi*.

**Origins and context.** Taoism emerged during China's Warring States period, offering an alternative to Confucian ritual and Legalist control. Laozi compared the ideal to water: soft, yet able to wear down stone.

**Other voices.** Edward Slingerland, *Trying Not to Try* (2014); Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's research on "flow" (1990).

**Related ideas.** Flow; Zen; minimalism.

**Pros.**
- Reduces anxious striving.
- Values humility and simplicity.

**Cons.**
- Offers few concrete duties.
- Can be misread as passivity.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Wu wei means doing nothing? That sounds like an excuse to be lazy.
>
> **Taoist:** It means not forcing. Cook Ding carved an ox for nineteen years without dulling his knife, because he followed the joints instead of hacking through bone.
>
> **Ari:** So it's about skill.
>
> **Taoist:** Skill, and attention. When you stop fighting the grain of things, effort becomes ease.
>
> **Ari:** How do I get there?
>
> **Taoist:** Usually through a lot of practice, and then letting go of the practice. Like a musician who no longer thinks about the notes.
>
> **Ari:** And in everyday life?
>
> **Taoist:** Notice where you're straining and ask if there's a gentler path around the obstacle. Water always finds one.

**Try it.**

- Pick a task you've been forcing and look for the "joints": an easier order, a better time of day, a different approach.
- Practise one skill until a part of it becomes effortless, then notice how that feels.

## 39. Former Belief: Life Is a Magnet

*Group: Your former belief · Told: 250 · Believed: 230 · Likely true: 20%*

**In summary.** Every situation has a positive and a negative side. Cut the positive half off, and it too has a positive and negative side, equally balanced, no matter how many times you divide it.

**In depth.**

This earlier belief pictured every situation as a magnet. Every magnet has a north and a south pole; cut it in half, and each half has both poles again, however many times you divide it. Likewise, the idea went, every situation has a positive and a negative side, and any part of it does too, perfectly balanced, at every level.

It drew on deep sources. Chinese yin-yang thought sees reality as the interplay of complementary opposites, each containing a seed of the other. The *Huainanzi* tells of an old farmer whose horse runs away: when neighbours call it bad luck, he says "maybe." The horse returns with wild horses (good luck?); his son breaks a leg riding one (bad luck?); and when war comes, the son is spared conscription because of the leg. Heraclitus held that opposites define each other, and Hegel built a philosophy of history on contradictions resolving into higher syntheses.

The belief had real virtues. It helped find the good in hard times, and it encouraged humility about judging events too quickly, since the story usually isn't over.

The author moved beyond it because, taken literally, it implies that improvement is pointless: if every gain produces an equal loss, nothing can get better overall. It also treats bad as equal and opposite to good, contradicting the later privation view, where bad is the absence of good rather than its mirror image. What survives is a gentler truth: every situation contains some good to find.

**Key figures.** Zou Yan (c. 305–240 BCE), associated with China's School of Yin-Yang; the authors of the *Huainanzi*.

**Key texts.** The *I Ching* (Book of Changes); the *Huainanzi* (139 BCE), which contains the parable of the old man who lost his horse.

**Origins and context.** Yin-yang thought developed in China's Warring States period, a time of chaos when thinkers sought patterns in constant change. In the *Huainanzi* parable, a farmer's horse runs away; when neighbors call it bad luck, he says "maybe." The horse returns with wild horses, his son breaks a leg riding one, and the injury spares the son from war. Each event splits again into good and bad.

**Other voices.** Heraclitus, who held that opposites define each other; Hegel's dialectic; Carl Jung on the shadow.

**Related ideas.** Physics: a magnet cut in half always yields two magnets, never a lone pole. Also "every cloud has a silver lining."

**Pros.**
- Helps people find the good in hard times.
- Encourages equanimity and humility about judging events quickly.

**Cons.**
- If every gain produces an equal loss, improvement seems pointless, undermining motivation.
- Treats bad as equal and opposite to good, contradicting the author's privation view.

**Worth knowing.** Reframed for the current system: *every situation contains some good to find.*

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Every situation is half good, half bad?
>
> **Author:** That was my old view. Like a magnet: cut it in half and both halves still have two poles.
>
> **Ari:** What was good about believing that?
>
> **Author:** It made me look for the upside in hard times, and slowed me down from judging too fast. Like the farmer whose bad luck kept turning out to be good.
>
> **Ari:** Why let it go?
>
> **Author:** If every gain makes an equal loss, nothing can ever really improve. I don't think that's true, and it's demotivating.
>
> **Ari:** So what replaced it?
>
> **Author:** Every situation has some good in it to find, and there's always more good to add. No law that it has to balance out.

**Try it.**

- Think of a recent setback and write down one good thing it contains or made possible.

## 40. Cartesian Skepticism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 250 · Believed: 250 · Likely true: 10%*

**In summary.** Doubt everything that can be doubted. The senses might deceive; you might be dreaming; an evil demon might be fooling you. The one thing you cannot doubt is that you are thinking, so you exist.

**In depth.**

Descartes wanted knowledge as certain as geometry, so in the *Meditations* he resolved to throw out every belief that could possibly be doubted, then see what survived. He climbed through three waves of doubt. First, the senses sometimes deceive us (a straight stick looks bent in water), so they can't be fully trusted. Second, dreams can feel completely real, and there's no sure sign to tell waking from dreaming. Third, and most radically, perhaps an all-powerful "evil demon" is deceiving him about everything, even mathematics.

What survives is the act of doubting itself. Even if a demon deceives me, there must be a "me" being deceived. *Cogito, ergo sum*: I think, therefore I am. From this single certainty Descartes tried to rebuild everything else, arguing that God exists, that a perfect God wouldn't systematically deceive us, and therefore that clear and distinct perceptions can be trusted. Few philosophers have found that rebuilding convincing; critics noted a circle, since he used clear perceptions to prove God and God to guarantee clear perceptions.

Descartes also split reality into two substances: mind, which thinks, and matter, which takes up space. This *dualism* set the agenda for centuries of debate about how mind and body interact, the "mind-body problem."

His skeptical scenarios have modern versions: Hilary Putnam's brain in a vat, wired to a computer that feeds it a perfect simulated world, and Nick Bostrom's simulation argument, which suggests that if advanced civilizations run many ancestor simulations, we might well be in one. Most philosophers today aren't global skeptics, but the method of systematic doubt, testing beliefs instead of inheriting them, shaped the scientific attitude. The book's low truth score reflects the radical conclusion, not the value of the method.

**Key figure.** René Descartes (1596–1650).

**Key texts.** *Discourse on the Method* (1637); *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641).

**Origins and context.** Descartes wrote during the Scientific Revolution and the devastating Thirty Years' War, shortly after Galileo's 1633 trial. Old authorities (Aristotle, the Church) were being questioned, and he sought an unshakable foundation for knowledge. Doubt was his method, not his conclusion.

**Other voices.** Pyrrho and Sextus Empiricus (ancient skeptics); David Hume; Hilary Putnam's brain in a vat (1981); Nick Bostrom's simulation argument (2003).

**Related ideas.** The scientific method; *The Matrix*; foundationalism.

**Pros.**
- Builds intellectual humility.
- Encourages checking assumptions.
- Helped launch modern philosophy.

**Cons.**
- Radical doubt about the external world is held by very few philosophers (under 5% in 2020).
- Little practical moral guidance.
- Descartes's escape route relied on proving God exists, which few find convincing.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** How do I know I'm not dreaming right now?
>
> **Cartesian:** That's exactly what Descartes asked. He couldn't find a sure test either.
>
> **Ari:** So I can't be sure of anything?
>
> **Cartesian:** One thing: that you're thinking. Even if you're dreaming or deceived, someone is doing the doubting. I think, therefore I am.
>
> **Ari:** That's not much to build a life on.
>
> **Cartesian:** Descartes tried to rebuild everything from it, with mixed success. But the method is worth keeping: doubt what you've inherited until you've tested it.
>
> **Ari:** Could we be in a simulation?
>
> **Cartesian:** Possibly. But notice: even then, your pain, your choices and the people you love would still matter to you. Doubt is a tool, not a home.

**Try it.**

- Pick one belief you've held since childhood and ask what evidence you'd have for it if you were meeting it today for the first time.

## 41. The Selfish Gene

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 250 · Believed: 260 · Likely true: 70%*

**In summary.** Evolution is best understood from the gene's point of view: organisms are vehicles that genes build to copy themselves. "Selfish" describes gene-level replication, not individual behavior; selfish genes can build generous, cooperative animals.

**In depth.**

Dawkins asked a question that had puzzled biologists since Darwin: if natural selection rewards survival and reproduction, why are animals ever generous? His answer was to change the unit of focus. Genes are the things that persist across generations, copying themselves through bodies that come and go. A gene that builds a body which helps relatives can spread, because relatives carry copies of the same gene. W. D. Hamilton formalized this as *kin selection*, summed up in "Hamilton's rule": an altruistic act is favoured when the benefit to the relative, multiplied by their relatedness, exceeds the cost to the helper. J. B. S. Haldane reportedly joked that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins.

Robert Trivers showed how altruism toward non-relatives can evolve too, through *reciprocal altruism*: helping those who will help you back, with emotions like gratitude, guilt and moral outrage keeping score. Vampire bats, which share blood meals with roostmates who have shared with them before, are a famous example. So "selfish genes" can produce genuinely cooperative, even self-sacrificing, animals; the selfishness is a metaphor for what gets copied, not a description of motives.

The book's final chapter introduced *memes*: units of culture, like tunes, ideas and habits, that spread by copying and evolve by a kind of selection. Dawkins also stressed that humans, uniquely, can rebel against their genes: "We, alone on earth, can rebel against the tyranny of the selfish replicators." Contraception is a simple example.

The gene's-eye view is now central to evolutionary biology, though debates continue. David Sloan Wilson and others argue that selection can act at several levels, including groups, and Stephen Jay Gould criticized over-reliance on adaptive stories. The most common misuse is moral: treating "selfish gene" as permission to be selfish. Dawkins himself warned against exactly that, since how things evolved says nothing about how we should live.

**Key figure.** Richard Dawkins (born 1941).

**Key text.** *The Selfish Gene* (1976).

**Origins and context.** Dawkins popularized work by W. D. Hamilton (kin selection, 1964), George C. Williams (*Adaptation and Natural Selection*, 1966), and Robert Trivers (reciprocal altruism, 1971), which explained altruism without group selection. The book also coined the word "meme."

**Other voices.** Hamilton; Williams; Trivers; critics Stephen Jay Gould, Mary Midgley, and David Sloan Wilson, who defends multilevel selection.

**Related ideas.** Kin selection; evolutionary psychology; memetics.

**Pros.**
- Explains altruism and cooperation scientifically.
- Dawkins himself argues humans can rebel against their genes.
- Central to modern evolutionary biology.

**Cons.**
- The title invites misreading as "selfishness is natural."
- Gene-centrism is debated by advocates of multilevel selection.
- Can be misused to justify selfish behavior.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If our genes are selfish, isn't kindness just fake?
>
> **Biologist:** No. "Selfish" describes how genes spread, not how you feel. Genes that build caring animals can spread very well.
>
> **Ari:** How?
>
> **Biologist:** Relatives share genes, so helping them helps copies of the same gene. And helping friends who help you back pays off over time.
>
> **Ari:** So I'm only kind because it helped my ancestors?
>
> **Biologist:** That's where the capacity came from. What you do with it is up to you. Dawkins said we're the only species that can rebel against our genes.
>
> **Ari:** What's a meme, originally?
>
> **Biologist:** Dawkins's word for an idea that spreads by copying, like a tune or a habit. Long before internet jokes.

**Try it.**

- Notice one kind act today that you did with no possible benefit to yourself, and appreciate that you can do that.

## 42. Absurdism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 250 · Believed: 260 · Likely true: 35%*

**In summary.** Humans crave meaning, but the universe offers none. This clash is the absurd. Rather than escape it through suicide or faith, embrace it with defiance: live fully, rebel, and find joy anyway.

**In depth.**

Camus begins *The Myth of Sisyphus* with a stark line: there is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide. Judging whether life is worth living, he says, is the fundamental question. His answer starts from a description of the human condition he calls *the absurd*. We long for meaning, clarity and unity; the universe answers with silence. The absurd is neither in us nor in the world but in the confrontation between them.

Camus considers three responses. Physical suicide ends the confrontation by ending the person. "Philosophical suicide," his name for leaping into faith or a system that promises meaning, ends it by denying one side. He rejects both. The third response is to live with the absurd fully awake, with *revolt* (refusing to be crushed), *freedom* (no longer bound by false hopes or eternal rules) and *passion* (living as intensely and fully as possible).

Sisyphus is his model. Condemned by the gods to push a boulder up a mountain forever, only to watch it roll down, Sisyphus knows his task is futile. Yet in the walk back down, conscious of his fate and refusing to submit to despair, he is superior to it. "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

In *The Rebel* and in novels like *The Plague*, Camus developed an ethics from this. Rebellion against meaninglessness implies solidarity: "I rebel, therefore we exist." He opposed both Nazism and Stalinist terror, breaking with Sartre over Sartre's sympathy for revolutionary violence, because he believed no abstract cause justifies murder. In *The Plague*, the doctor Rieux fights an epidemic not from hope of ultimate victory but because it is the decent thing to do.

**Key figure.** Albert Camus (1913–1960).

**Key texts.** *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942); *The Stranger* (1942); *The Rebel* (1951).

**Origins and context.** Camus, born in French Algeria to a poor family, wrote during World War II while working with the Resistance newspaper *Combat*. He won the Nobel Prize in 1957. His image of Sisyphus, endlessly pushing a boulder uphill, ends with the claim that we must imagine Sisyphus happy.

**Other voices.** Kierkegaard (who coined related ideas but chose faith); Sartre (with whom Camus split in 1952); Thomas Nagel's essay "The Absurd" (1971).

**Related ideas.** Existentialism; nihilism; Stoicism.

**Pros.**
- Finds joy without needing ultimate answers.
- Rejects despair and fanaticism alike.
- Camus's ethics opposed violence and tyranny.

**Cons.**
- "Meaningless universe" can discourage some people.
- Offers defiance more than guidance.
- Rebellion without direction can drift.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If the universe has no meaning, why bother doing anything?
>
> **Absurdist:** Camus asked exactly that. His answer: because you're alive, and you can choose to live fully anyway.
>
> **Ari:** Isn't that just pretending?
>
> **Absurdist:** No pretending. You look straight at the meaninglessness and refuse to be crushed by it. That's what he called revolt.
>
> **Ari:** Sisyphus pushing his rock forever seems miserable.
>
> **Absurdist:** Camus said we must imagine him happy. The struggle itself can fill a heart.
>
> **Ari:** And how should I treat others in a meaningless world?

> **Absurdist:** As fellow rebels. We're all in the same absurd situation, which is a reason for solidarity, not cruelty.

## 43. Evolutionary Ethics

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 240 · Believed: 230 · Likely true: 50%*

**In summary.** Morality is a product of evolution. Descriptively, our moral instincts (fairness, loyalty, care) evolved because they helped ancestors survive in groups. Prescriptively, some have argued that whatever aids survival is good, a much more contested claim.

**In depth.**

Evolutionary ethics has two very different halves, and the book's wide range reflects that. The *descriptive* half asks where moral feelings came from. Darwin suggested in *The Descent of Man* that any animal with strong social instincts and enough intelligence would develop a moral sense. Modern research supports much of this. Capuchin monkeys in Frans de Waal's experiments reject cucumber when a neighbour gets grapes for the same task, an apparent sense of fairness. Chimpanzees console victims of aggression. Game theory shows how cooperation, reciprocity and punishment of cheaters can evolve. Jonathan Haidt's moral foundations (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty) look like evolved intuitions shaped by different cultures.

The *prescriptive* half makes a much bigger claim: that whatever evolution favours is therefore good. Herbert Spencer, who coined "survival of the fittest," built a social philosophy on this, arguing that helping the poor interfered with natural progress. "Social Darwinism" was later used to justify colonialism, laissez-faire neglect and eugenics, culminating in some of the twentieth century's worst crimes.

Philosophers have long identified the logical problem. David Hume observed that you can't derive an "ought" from an "is": no list of facts about what happens tells you what should happen. G. E. Moore called the attempt to define goodness as any natural property the "naturalistic fallacy." Thomas Huxley, Darwin's great defender, argued in 1893 that ethics should *resist* the cosmic process, not imitate it: civilized morality consists in protecting the weak that nature would discard. Peter Kropotkin, meanwhile, pointed out that evolution favours mutual aid as much as competition.

The descriptive half is valuable, explaining why moral instincts exist and why they can misfire, such as in-group bias. The prescriptive half is where the danger lies.

**Key figures.** Charles Darwin (1809–1882); Herbert Spencer (1820–1903).

**Key texts.** Darwin's *The Descent of Man* (1871); E. O. Wilson's *Sociobiology* (1975); Frans de Waal's *Primates and Philosophers* (2006).

**Origins and context.** Darwin suggested the moral sense arose from social instincts. Spencer, who coined "survival of the fittest" in 1864, built "social Darwinism," used in the Gilded Age to justify neglect of the poor and later eugenics. Thomas Huxley argued in "Evolution and Ethics" (1893) that ethics should *resist* the cosmic process. Peter Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid* (1902) emphasized cooperation.

**Other voices.** Kropotkin; Richard Joyce, *The Evolution of Morality* (2006); Jonathan Haidt.

**Related ideas.** The is-ought gap (Hume); the naturalistic fallacy (G. E. Moore, 1903); the author's motivations principle.

**Pros.**
- Explains why moral instincts exist and why they vary.
- Fosters understanding of human behavior.
- Supported by primate and game-theory research.

**Cons.**
- Prescriptive versions slid into social Darwinism and eugenics.
- Explaining a trait's origin doesn't make it good.
- Wide range: 100–300 sparkles depending on which version a person holds.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Did morality evolve?
>
> **Evolutionary psychologist:** Our moral instincts almost certainly did. Monkeys protest unfair pay, and chimps comfort each other after fights.
>
> **Ari:** So whatever helps survival is good?
>
> **Evolutionary psychologist:** That's the dangerous leap. "Survival of the fittest" was used to justify letting the poor suffer, and worse.
>
> **Ari:** Why is that a mistake?
>
> **Evolutionary psychologist:** Explaining where a feeling came from doesn't tell you whether to follow it. Evolution also gave us tribalism and revenge.
>
> **Ari:** Then what's the use of knowing all this?
>
> **Evolutionary psychologist:** It helps you understand your instincts, including the ones worth overriding. Huxley put it well: ethics often means resisting nature, not copying it.

**Try it.**

- Notice one instinctive reaction today, like distrust of an outsider or a pull toward revenge, and ask whether it fits the situation or only the ancestral one.

## 44. Moral Subjectivity

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 230 · Believed: 230 · Likely true: 26%*

**In summary.** Moral judgments express feelings, attitudes, or cultural conventions rather than objective facts. "Murder is wrong" is more like "I strongly disapprove of murder" than like "water is H₂O."

**In depth.**

Moral subjectivism and its relatives hold that moral claims don't describe objective facts. Within this family there are several distinct views. *Simple subjectivism* says "murder is wrong" means "I disapprove of murder." *Cultural relativism* says it means "my society disapproves of murder." *Emotivism*, championed by A. J. Ayer, says moral statements aren't statements at all but expressions of feeling, like saying "murder, boo!" *Error theory*, J. L. Mackie's view, says moral claims do try to state objective facts, but there are no such facts, so they're all false.

The roots go back to Hume, who argued that reason alone can only tell us what is the case; it takes feeling to move us. "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions." Vice, he said, can't be found in any act you examine; you find it only when you turn to your own feelings of disapproval.

The appeal is real. Moral views do vary across cultures and history. Subjectivism encourages humility and tolerance: if no one has the final moral truth, perhaps we should be slower to condemn. It also fits a scientific picture of the world in which values don't appear among the physical facts.

The problems are serious, which is why most philosophers reject strong versions. If morality is only preference, then "slavery was wrong" means only that we dislike it, and the abolitionists weren't *right*, just different. Moral progress becomes mere change, and moral disagreement becomes as pointless as arguing over favourite ice-cream flavours. Relativism also undermines its own tolerance: if a culture values intolerance, relativism gives no grounds to object. Sophisticated anti-realists, like Simon Blackburn's "quasi-realism," try to explain how we can still talk about moral truth and progress without objective moral facts.

**Key figures.** David Hume (1711–1776); J. L. Mackie (1917–1981).

**Key texts.** Hume's *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40); A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936); Mackie's *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977).

**Origins and context.** The ancient Greek Protagoras held that "man is the measure of all things." Hume argued that reason alone can't produce moral conclusions; feelings do. In the twentieth century, logical positivists like Ayer called moral statements expressions of emotion, and Mackie argued moral claims are systematically false.

**Other voices.** Protagoras; Edvard Westermarck; Gilbert Harman.

**Related ideas.** Relativism; emotivism; error theory.

**Pros.**
- Encourages tolerance of different cultures.
- Fits the observation that morality varies.
- Humble about moral certainty.

**Cons.**
- Makes it hard to condemn atrocities.
- About 26% of philosophers favored anti-realism in 2020.
- Some evidence suggests subjectivist framing slightly reduces moral motivation.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Who's to say what's right or wrong? Isn't it all opinion?
>
> **Subjectivist:** I think morality comes from human feelings and cultures, not from facts out in the universe.
>
> **Ari:** So was slavery fine back when people accepted it?
>
> **Subjectivist:** That's the hardest question for my view. I'd say I condemn it with everything I have, but I can't point to a cosmic fact that makes me right.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that make morality pointless?
>
> **Subjectivist:** No. My feelings about cruelty are real, and they move me. Most people share them, so we can still build laws and argue.
>
> **Ari:** What's the upside of your view?
>
> **Subjectivist:** Humility. It's harder to persecute people when you remember your values aren't carved into the stars.

**Try it.**

- Find a moral belief you hold that varies a lot between cultures, and one that seems nearly universal, and consider what explains the difference.

## 45. Anarchism

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 230 (range 100–320) · Believed: 230 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Coercive hierarchy, including the state, is illegitimate; society should run on voluntary cooperation.

**In depth.**

Anarchism's name comes from the Greek *anarkhia*, "without a ruler." Anarchists argue that coercive hierarchy, power some people hold over others without their consent, is illegitimate unless it can justify itself, and that the state, which claims a monopoly on force, rarely can. Proudhon's provocative line "property is theft" targeted the ownership of land and capital that lets some live off others' labour, though he defended personal possessions. Anarchism is not chaos in its theorists' eyes; it is order without rulers, built from voluntary association, federation and mutual aid.

Peter Kropotkin, a Russian prince turned geographer, gave it a scientific argument. In *Mutual Aid* he showed, from animal behaviour and human history, that cooperation was as important to survival as competition, from medieval guilds to village communes. Human beings, he argued, can organize themselves without states, and often do.

Anarchism has many strands. Anarcho-communists and anarcho-syndicalists favour collective ownership and worker-run unions; the Spanish revolution of 1936 saw parts of Catalonia run this way for a time. Individualist anarchists emphasize personal sovereignty. Tolstoy's Christian anarchism rejected all violence, including the state's. Anarcho-capitalists favour private property with private protection.

The wide score range, 100 to 320, reflects this diversity. Peaceful anarchism, focused on mutual aid, community self-help and suspicion of concentrated power, can inspire a great deal of good, and anarchist ideas shaped many cooperatives and grassroots movements. But some anarchists of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries embraced "propaganda of the deed," assassinating several heads of state, and large stateless societies have rarely lasted against outside military pressure or internal conflict.

**Key figure.** Pierre-Joseph Proudhon (1809–1865), the first to call himself an anarchist.

**Key texts.** Proudhon's *What Is Property?* (1840); Peter Kropotkin's *The Conquest of Bread* (1892) and *Mutual Aid* (1902).

**Origins and context.** Anarchism emerged amid industrialization and nineteenth-century revolutions. Anarchists ran parts of Catalonia during the Spanish Civil War (1936).

**Other voices.** Mikhail Bakunin; Emma Goldman; Leo Tolstoy's Christian anarchism; James C. Scott, *Two Cheers for Anarchism* (2012).

**Related ideas.** Mutual aid; libertarian socialism; anarcho-capitalism.

**Pros.**
- Highlights the dangers of concentrated power.
- Peaceful strains emphasize mutual aid.

**Cons.**
- Some strains embraced violence: a self-described anarchist assassinated US President McKinley in 1901.
- Few large-scale examples have lasted.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Anarchy means chaos, right?
>
> **Anarchist:** Most anarchists mean the opposite: order that comes from people cooperating freely, not from rulers.
>
> **Ari:** Who would build the roads?
>
> **Anarchist:** The same people who build them now, organized through communities, co-ops and federations instead of a state. People cooperate constantly without being ordered to.
>
> **Ari:** What about violent anarchists?
>
> **Anarchist:** A real and ugly part of the history. Many of us reject violence completely; Tolstoy was an anarchist and a pacifist.
>
> **Ari:** Has it ever worked?
>
> **Anarchist:** In pieces: mutual aid networks, cooperatives, parts of Spain in 1936 for a while. Even if you never want to abolish the state, it's worth asking of every power: what justifies it?

**Try it.**

- Look for a mutual-aid group, co-op or community project near you, and see how it organizes without a boss.

## 46. Killing to Eat

*Group: Action · Told: 225 (range 150–300) · Believed: 225 · Likely true: not assessed (an action)*

**In summary.** Killing animals for food, from subsistence hunting to industrial farming. Its score depends heavily on necessity and method.

**In depth.**

"Killing to eat" covers a huge range of practices, which is why the book gives it a wide range rather than a single score. At one end is subsistence hunting and fishing by people with no realistic alternative, such as Arctic communities where plants barely grow. At the other end is industrial animal farming in wealthy countries, where most people could eat well without it. Between them lie small farms with high welfare standards, ethical hunting, and fishing.

Necessity is the first question. Where animal food is needed for survival or health, killing to eat sits high on the scale. Where alternatives are easy, its score depends heavily on the second question: how much suffering is involved. Industrial systems raise tens of billions of land animals a year, many in confinement that prevents natural behaviour, with breeding that causes chronic pain, such as broiler chickens that grow so fast their legs can fail. Well-run small farms and hunting can involve far less suffering, and a skilled hunter's clean shot may cause less pain than many natural deaths.

Aldo Leopold, a hunter and conservationist, argued for a "land ethic" that sees humans as members of an ecological community rather than its conquerors. Michael Pollan's *The Omnivore's Dilemma* traced four meals, from fast food to a meal he hunted and foraged himself, and concluded that most people would eat very differently if they saw how their food was made. Temple Grandin, an animal scientist, redesigned the handling systems used for a large share of US cattle to reduce fear and stress at slaughter, showing that incremental reform can reduce suffering at scale.

Many Indigenous traditions treat hunting with ritual gratitude and strict norms against waste, a reminder that eating animals can be done with respect. The ethical pressure in this chapter comes mostly from scale and necessity: industrial farming where alternatives exist is the low end of the range.

**Key figures.** Aldo Leopold (1887–1948); Michael Pollan (born 1955).

**Key texts.** Leopold's *A Sand County Almanac* (1949); Pollan's *The Omnivore's Dilemma* (2006).

**Origins and context.** Hunting sustained humans for hundreds of thousands of years. Many Indigenous traditions practice gratitude toward hunted animals. Industrial farming since the mid-twentieth century transformed scale and suffering.

**Other voices.** Temple Grandin, who redesigned livestock handling to reduce stress.

**Pros.**
- Necessary for survival in some places.
- Hunting can be part of ecological management.

**Cons.**
- Factory farming causes massive suffering when alternatives exist.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Is eating meat wrong?
>
> **Farmer:** Depends a lot on how it's raised and whether you need it. A hunter in the Arctic and a factory farm aren't the same thing.
>
> **Ari:** What makes the difference?
>
> **Farmer:** Suffering, mainly. My animals live outdoors and die quickly. In the biggest farms, many animals live their whole lives crowded or confined.
>
> **Ari:** Should everyone become vegetarian?
>
> **Farmer:** That's for you to weigh. But if you eat meat, eat less of it, know where it comes from, and waste none of it.
>
> **Ari:** Does one person's choice matter?
>
> **Farmer:** Every purchase is a vote for how animals are raised. Farms like mine exist because people choose them.

**Try it.**

- Find out where the meat you most often eat comes from and how the animals were raised.
- If you eat meat, try making one higher-welfare or plant-based swap a week.

## 47. Tit for Tat

*Group: Strategy · Told: 220 · Believed: 230 · Likely true: not assessed (a strategy)*

**In summary.** Start by cooperating, then copy whatever the other party did last. Reward cooperation, punish defection, and forgive immediately once they cooperate again.

**In depth.**

The Prisoner's Dilemma is a simple game with a painful logic. Two players each choose to cooperate or defect. If both cooperate, both do well. If one defects while the other cooperates, the defector does best and the cooperator worst. If both defect, both do poorly. In a single round, defecting is always tempting, so rational players end up worse off than if they had trusted each other.

Robert Axelrod's insight was that real life is usually a *repeated* game: we meet the same people again. In 1980 he ran two computer tournaments in which strategies submitted by game theorists, economists and psychologists played many rounds against each other. Anatol Rapoport's tit for tat, just four lines of code, won both. It cooperates on the first move, then does whatever the opponent did last.

Axelrod identified four properties behind its success. It is *nice*: it never defects first. It is *retaliatory*: it punishes defection immediately, so it can't be exploited for long. It is *forgiving*: once the other side cooperates again, it goes back to cooperating. And it is *clear*: opponents quickly learn that cooperation pays. Axelrod also found examples in the real world, such as the "live and let live" truces that sprang up between opposing trenches in World War I, where units avoided shooting at each other as long as the other side held back.

Tit for tat has a known weakness: noise. If one player defects by mistake, or a cooperative move is misread, two tit-for-tat players can lock into an endless echo of retaliation. Later research by Martin Nowak and Karl Sigmund found that "generous tit for tat," which occasionally forgives a defection, and "win-stay, lose-shift" can do better in noisy worlds. As a personal strategy it scores in the middle: reliable and fair, but less generous than strategies that sometimes give first or forgive more.

**Key figures.** Anatol Rapoport (1911–2007), who submitted the strategy; Robert Axelrod (born 1943), who ran the tournaments.

**Key text.** Axelrod's *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984).

**Origins and context.** In 1980, during Cold War nuclear standoffs, Axelrod invited experts to submit computer programs to play repeated rounds of the Prisoner's Dilemma. Rapoport's tit for tat, the simplest entry, won both tournaments. Axelrod concluded successful strategies are nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear.

**Other voices.** William Hamilton, Axelrod's coauthor on its biological applications; Martin Nowak, whose later work found "generous" variants that occasionally forgive do even better.

**Related ideas.** Reciprocal altruism; game theory; deterrence.

**Pros.**
- Proven to foster cooperation among self-interested players.
- Simple and transparent.
- Quick to forgive.

**Cons.**
- A single misunderstanding can lock two players into endless retaliation.
- Never initiates generosity beyond the first move.
- Assumes repeated interactions.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** What's the best strategy when you don't know if someone can be trusted?
>
> **Game theorist:** Start by cooperating. If they cheat you, respond. If they come back, forgive.
>
> **Ari:** That's it?
>
> **Game theorist:** That simple rule beat dozens of clever strategies in Axelrod's tournaments. Nice, retaliatory, forgiving and clear.
>
> **Ari:** What's the catch?
>
> **Game theorist:** Misunderstandings. If you mistake an accident for an attack, two tit-for-tat players can trade blows forever.
>
> **Ari:** How do you avoid that?
>
> **Game theorist:** Be a little more generous than strict tit for tat. Forgive now and then, especially when it might have been an accident. That does even better.

**Try it.**

- In a relationship where things have gone sour, make one cooperative move first and see if it's returned.
- Before retaliating for a slight, ask whether it could have been an accident.

## 48. Original Sin

*Group: Religious message · Told: 220 · Believed: 200 · Likely true: not assessed (faith-based)*

**In summary.** Humanity inherits a fallen, sinful nature from Adam and Eve's disobedience, so every person begins in need of redemption.

**In depth.**

In Genesis, Adam and Eve disobey God by eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, and are expelled from Eden. Paul later interpreted this in Romans: "sin entered the world through one man," and death through sin, so that all people share in the fall. Augustine developed this into the doctrine of original sin: humanity inherits not only death but a corrupted will, inclined toward pride and selfishness, and even guilt itself. Humans cannot become good by their own effort; they need God's grace.

Augustine's opponent, the British monk Pelagius, argued that humans are born innocent and can choose good freely, with Adam's sin offering only a bad example. The Western church sided with Augustine, condemning Pelagianism in 418. Later, John Calvin pressed the doctrine further into "total depravity": every part of human nature is touched by sin, though not every person is as bad as possible.

Not all traditions agree. Judaism teaches that people are born with both a good and an evil inclination (*yetzer hatov* and *yetzer hara*) and can choose. Islam holds that Adam and Eve sinned, repented and were forgiven, and that every child is born in a natural state of purity (*fitra*). Eastern Orthodoxy speaks of "ancestral sin": inheriting mortality and a tendency to sin, but not personal guilt.

The doctrine has a realistic side. Reinhold Niebuhr argued that groups behave worse than individuals and that politics must account for human self-interest, an outlook that shaped twentieth-century realism. James Madison's checks and balances rest on similar caution: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary." The cost the book highlights is psychological: teaching people, especially children, that they are inherently bad tends to produce shame rather than the guilt that motivates repair.

**Key figure.** Augustine of Hippo.

**Key texts.** Genesis 3; Romans 5:12; Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings; John Calvin's *Institutes* (1536).

**Origins and context.** Augustine developed the doctrine in the early fifth century against Pelagius, who taught humans could choose good unaided. The Council of Carthage (418) sided with Augustine. Judaism and Islam reject inherited guilt, and Eastern Orthodoxy teaches a milder "ancestral sin."

**Other voices.** Calvin; Reinhold Niebuhr, *Moral Man and Immoral Society* (1932); G. K. Chesterton, who joked in *Orthodoxy* (1908) that it is the one doctrine that can be proven by observation.

**Related ideas.** Self-discrepancy theory; checks and balances (James Madison's argument in Federalist No. 51 that government is needed because people are not angels).

**Pros.**
- Realism about human failings; supports institutional checks on power.
- Encourages humility.

**Cons.**
- Locates badness in the self, which June Tangney's research links to shame, defensiveness, and resentment rather than repair.
- Can make people feel inherently bad from birth, the opposite of the author's philosophy.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** So I'm born guilty for something someone else did?
>
> **Theologian:** Augustine thought so. Many Christians today put it differently: we're born with a tendency to put ourselves first, and we all fall short.
>
> **Ari:** That seems unfair.
>
> **Theologian:** It's one of the most debated teachings in Christianity. Judaism and Islam reject inherited guilt altogether.
>
> **Ari:** What's the point of believing it?
>
> **Theologian:** Realism. It explains why even good people do harm, and why power needs checks. Madison said if people were angels, no government would be needed.
>
> **Ari:** But telling kids they're sinful from birth?
>
> **Theologian:** That's the danger. The teaching was meant to lead people to grace, not to make them feel worthless.

**Try it.**

- Notice whether your self-talk after a mistake says "I did something wrong" or "I am bad," and practise the first.

## 49. Nihilism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 200 · Believed: 180 · Likely true: 15%*

**In summary.** Life has no inherent meaning, purpose, or value, and moral claims have no foundation.

**In depth.**

Nihilism comes from the Latin *nihil*, "nothing." In its strongest form it holds that nothing has inherent value: life has no meaning, morality has no foundation, and knowledge may have none either. The word spread through Ivan Turgenev's 1862 novel *Fathers and Sons*, whose young character Bazarov rejects every authority and tradition, believing only in what science can prove.

Nietzsche is often called a nihilist, but he was mainly its diagnostician. In *The Gay Science* a madman announces that "God is dead, and we have killed him." Nietzsche's point was cultural: Europe's belief in God, which had grounded its morality and sense of meaning for centuries, was collapsing, and most people hadn't realized what would fall with it. He feared a coming age of nihilism, "the uncanniest of all guests," and hoped humans would respond by creating new values: affirming life fully, even to the point of willing it to recur eternally.

Philosophers distinguish several kinds. *Existential nihilism* says life has no meaning. *Moral nihilism* says nothing is morally right or wrong. *Epistemological nihilism* says knowledge is impossible. Each has been answered in different ways: existentialists say meaning is created, not found; absurdists say we can live fully without ultimate meaning; moral realists argue there are moral truths after all.

Dostoevsky dramatized the stakes. In *The Brothers Karamazov* the reasoning that "if there is no God, everything is permitted" leads to murder, and in *Demons* nihilist revolutionaries destroy one another. The book scores nihilism low, and lower still when fully believed, because it removes many of the reasons people have for effort and kindness. Its honest value is as a challenge: it forces us to ask what our values actually rest on.

**Key figures.** Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900), who diagnosed nihilism rather than endorsing it; Ivan Turgenev, who popularized the word.

**Key texts.** Turgenev's novel *Fathers and Sons* (1862); Nietzsche's *The Gay Science* (1882), with its "death of God" passage.

**Origins and context.** The term spread through 1860s Russia, where young radicals rejected tradition, religion, and authority. Nietzsche saw nihilism as the coming crisis of a Europe losing its religious foundations, and hoped humanity would create new values to overcome it.

**Other voices.** Emil Cioran; Dostoevsky, who explored its dangers in *Demons* and *The Brothers Karamazov*; Ray Brassier.

**Related ideas.** Existentialism and absurdism (both responses to it); moral error theory.

**Pros.**
- Can feel liberating from imposed expectations.
- Forces honest examination of values.

**Cons.**
- Removes common motivations for effort and kindness.
- Associated with despair for some.
- Most philosophers reject it; most of its famous discussers sought to overcome it.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Sometimes I feel like nothing matters.
>
> **Philosopher:** That feeling has a name, and serious thinkers have faced it. Nietzsche saw it coming for all of Europe.
>
> **Ari:** Did he think nothing matters?
>
> **Philosopher:** He thought the old reasons were collapsing. His hope was that people would create new values and say yes to life anyway.
>
> **Ari:** How?
>
> **Philosopher:** Notice that you still prefer kindness to cruelty, music to silence, friends to loneliness. Those preferences are real, even if the universe doesn't sign off on them.
>
> **Ari:** And if the feeling won't lift?
>
> **Philosopher:** Then talk to someone, a friend or a professional. Persistent emptiness can be depression rather than philosophy, and it's very treatable.

**Try it.**

- List five things that matter to you even on a bad day, and why.
- If feelings that nothing matters persist for weeks, talk to a doctor or counsellor.

## 50. Communism (Marx's Ideal)

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 200 (range 100–300) · Believed: 200 · Likely true: not assessed (values plus contested empirics)*

**Root assumption.** Society can be remade into a classless, stateless community where people give according to ability and receive according to need.

**In depth.**

Marx's ideal was a society without classes, private ownership of the means of production, or a state. He argued that all history is a history of class struggle: masters and slaves, lords and serfs, and in his time, capitalists who own factories and workers who own only their labour. Under capitalism, he said, workers are paid less than the value they produce, with the difference, *surplus value*, going to owners as profit. Workers are also *alienated*: cut off from the products of their work, from meaningful activity, from each other and from their human potential.

Marx predicted that capitalism's contradictions would lead to crises and revolution. Workers would take control, pass through a transitional stage he called the dictatorship of the proletariat, and eventually build communism, where the state "withers away." Distribution would follow the principle "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs." Freed from drudgery, people could, in his and Engels's playful image, hunt in the morning, fish in the afternoon and criticize after dinner.

As a critique, Marx's work was enormously influential. His analysis of how economic interests shape politics and culture, his account of capitalism's booms and crashes, and his focus on workers' conditions influenced economics, sociology and labour movements that won the weekend, workplace safety laws and the end of child labour.

As a blueprint, it was vague, and the book separates the ideal from its practice. The ideal scores in a wide middle range: its vision of shared abundance is generous, but its division of people into oppressors and oppressed invites dehumanization, and economists from Ludwig von Mises to Friedrich Hayek argued that without market prices, no planner can allocate resources efficiently. Small voluntary communes like Israel's early kibbutzim worked for a time; attempts to impose the vision on whole nations are the next chapter's subject.

**Key figures.** Karl Marx (1818–1883); Friedrich Engels (1820–1895).

**Key texts.** *The Communist Manifesto* (1848); *Capital*, Volume I (1867); *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875).

**Origins and context.** Marx and Engels wrote during brutal early industrialization; Engels documented factory conditions in *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845). The 1848 revolutions swept Europe as the *Manifesto* appeared.

**Other voices.** Utopian socialists Robert Owen and Charles Fourier; the early Christian communities described in Acts 2–4; Israel's kibbutzim (from 1910).

**Related ideas.** Socialism; labor movements; communal living.

**Pros.**
- Sharp critique of exploitation; influenced labor laws and workers' rights.
- Generous moral vision.

**Cons.**
- Dividing people into oppressor and oppressed classes invites dehumanization.
- Ludwig von Mises (1920) argued planned economies cannot calculate prices efficiently.
- Doubts about incentives and human nature.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** What did Marx actually want?
>
> **Historian:** A world without classes, where people share what's produced and nobody lives off others' work.
>
> **Ari:** That doesn't sound evil.
>
> **Historian:** As an ideal, it's generous. "From each according to ability, to each according to need."
>
> **Ari:** So why did it go so wrong?
>
> **Historian:** Marx said little about how such a society would actually work. And treating whole classes of people as enemies made violence easy to justify.
>
> **Ari:** Is there anything worth keeping?
>
> **Historian:** His questions. Who owns what, who does the work, and who gets the benefit? Labour movements used those questions to win much of what workers have today.

**Try it.**

- Pick something you own and think about whose labour made it, and how much of its price they likely received.

## 51. Ethical Egoism

*Group: Philosophy · Told: 180 · Believed: 170 · Likely true: 5%*

**In summary.** The right action is whatever serves your own long-term self-interest. Helping others is good only insofar as it benefits you.

**In depth.**

Ethical egoism holds that each person ought to pursue their own long-term self-interest. It is a claim about what you *should* do, unlike psychological egoism, the descriptive claim that people always do act selfishly, which most psychologists reject: people regularly sacrifice for others in ways no self-interest explains.

Ethical egoism usually means *enlightened* self-interest. A smart egoist keeps promises, builds friendships, and avoids cruelty, because these tend to serve long-term interests. Ayn Rand, its best-known modern advocate, called selfishness a virtue, by which she meant rational self-interest: pursuing your own values, producing rather than taking, and trading with others as equals rather than sacrificing yourself or demanding sacrifices. Her novels celebrated independent creators, such as architects, industrialists and inventors, against what she saw as the guilt-driven ethics of self-sacrifice and collectivism. Max Stirner, a century earlier, went further, rejecting every ideal above the individual, including morality itself, as a "spook."

Egoism has a few genuine strengths: it takes self-respect seriously, rejects ethics that demand people destroy themselves for others, and is honest that self-interest motivates much of life.

But it faces serious objections, which is why so few philosophers defend it. It gives no reason to help someone when helping costs you and nobody will ever know. It seems arbitrary: why should *my* interests count for more simply because they're mine, when others' suffering is just as real? James Rachels compared it to racism, privileging one group (yourself) without a relevant difference. And it seems to fail even by its own measure: research consistently finds that people who give and care for others report greater happiness than those focused on themselves.

**Key figures.** Max Stirner (1806–1856); Ayn Rand (1905–1982).

**Key texts.** Stirner's *The Ego and Its Own* (1844); Rand's *Atlas Shrugged* (1957) and *The Virtue of Selfishness* (1964).

**Origins and context.** Stirner wrote among German Young Hegelians rejecting all abstractions, including God and the state. Rand, who fled Soviet Russia after the revolution, developed Objectivism as a defense of individual achievement against collectivism, gaining wide influence in mid-century America.

**Other voices.** Thomas Hobbes (psychological egoism); Henry Sidgwick, who took egoism seriously; critics James Rachels and Kurt Baier.

**Related ideas.** Libertarianism; psychological egoism; enlightened self-interest.

**Pros.**
- Encourages self-respect and ambition.
- Honest about self-interest.
- Rejects exploitative self-sacrifice.

**Cons.**
- Offers no reason to help others when it costs you.
- Very few philosophers defend it.
- Can rationalize callousness.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Isn't everyone selfish deep down?
>
> **Egoist:** Not everyone. But I think everyone *should* pursue their own long-term interest, intelligently.
>
> **Ari:** So you'd never help anyone?
>
> **Egoist:** I help friends constantly. Good relationships are in my interest.
>
> **Ari:** What about a stranger drowning, when nobody would ever know?
>
> **Egoist:** That's the hard case for my view. A strict egoist has no reason to help if it costs them.
>
> **Ari:** Doesn't that bother you?
>
> **Egoist:** Honestly, it's where most people stop agreeing with Rand. And the research says generous people tend to be happier, so even selfishly, kindness pays.

**Try it.**

- Do something kind for someone who can't repay you, and notice how you feel afterwards.

## 52. An Eye for an Eye

*Group: Strategy · Told: 150 · Believed: 140 · Likely true: not assessed (a strategy)*

**In summary.** Punishment should match the harm done: no more, no less. Originally a *limit* on revenge.

**In depth.**

*Lex talionis*, the law of retaliation, sounds harsh today, but it began as a limit. In societies without courts, an injury to one family member could spark a feud that escalated until whole clans were destroyed. The principle "eye for eye, tooth for tooth" set a ceiling: the response could not exceed the harm. The Code of Hammurabi applied it, though unequally: harming a nobleman brought equal harm in return, while harming a commoner or a slave usually meant only a fine.

The Hebrew Bible states the rule in Exodus, Leviticus and Deuteronomy. Rabbinic tradition, recorded in the Talmud, concluded early that it should not be applied literally but meant monetary compensation equal to the value of the loss, covering damages, pain, medical costs, lost work and humiliation. The Quran includes the principle in 5:45 and immediately adds that whoever forgoes retaliation as charity, it will be an atonement for them, encouraging forgiveness and payment instead.

Proportionality survives at the heart of modern law: punishments should fit crimes, and self-defence must be proportionate to the threat. Retributive justice, the idea that wrongdoers deserve punishment matching their wrong, remains one of the main justifications for criminal sentences.

The book scores the strategy low because, as a personal guide, it keeps attention on inflicting harm rather than repairing it. It doesn't heal victims, it can still escalate when the two sides disagree about what counts as equal, and it treats the other person mainly as a debt to be collected. Restorative justice and forgiveness, which the book scores higher, aim at repair instead of balance.

**Key figure.** Hammurabi, king of Babylon (reigned c. 1792–1750 BCE).

**Key texts.** The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE); Exodus 21:23–25; the Quran 5:45.

**Origins and context.** In societies without police or courts, feuds could escalate endlessly: a killing answered by a massacre. Proportional punishment, known as *lex talionis*, was progress. Rabbinic tradition later interpreted the biblical rule as monetary compensation, and the Quran pairs it with encouragement to forgive.

**Other voices.** The Talmud's discussion in tractate Bava Kamma; the phrase "an eye for an eye makes the whole world blind," often attributed to Gandhi, though the attribution is uncertain.

**Related ideas.** Retributive justice; proportionality in law; tit for tat.

**Pros.**
- Limits escalation.
- Intuitive sense of fairness.
- Laid groundwork for proportional legal systems.

**Cons.**
- Still centers on inflicting harm.
- Doesn't repair victims.
- Literal versions are cruel.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If someone hurts me, isn't it fair to hurt them back equally?
>
> **Judge:** That rule was progress once. It stopped a slap turning into a massacre.
>
> **Ari:** So it's fair?
>
> **Judge:** It's limited. But who decides what's equal? Each side usually thinks the other went further, and the cycle continues.
>
> **Ari:** Then what's better?
>
> **Judge:** Even the ancient rabbis turned it into compensation: pay for the harm instead of repeating it. Modern courts aim for proportion, and restorative justice aims for repair.
>
> **Ari:** And personally?
>
> **Judge:** Ask what would actually make things better, not what would make things even.

**Try it.**

- When you feel the urge to "get even," write down what would actually repair the harm, and pursue that instead.

## 53. Marxist-Leninist Practice

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 50 · Believed: 30 · Likely true: 10%*

**Root assumption.** A vanguard party must seize the state and rule on behalf of workers until communism is achieved.

**In depth.**

Lenin adapted Marx's theory to a country Marx never expected to lead a revolution: Russia, mostly peasant and barely industrialized. His key innovation, set out in *What Is to Be Done?*, was the *vanguard party*: a disciplined organization of professional revolutionaries who would lead workers, since he believed workers left alone would settle for better wages rather than overthrowing capitalism. After the Bolsheviks seized power in 1917, the "dictatorship of the proletariat" became, in practice, the dictatorship of the party, which banned other parties, created the Cheka secret police and launched the Red Terror.

Under Stalin the system hardened. Forced collectivization of agriculture destroyed peasant farming; the resulting famines killed millions, including the Holodomor in Ukraine. The Great Terror of 1936–38 executed hundreds of thousands, and the Gulag labour camps held millions. Mao's China followed a similar path: the Great Leap Forward's impossible production targets and false reporting led to a famine that killed tens of millions, and the Cultural Revolution turned students against teachers and children against parents. In Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge emptied cities and killed perhaps a fifth of the population in under four years.

Why did a philosophy of liberation produce such suffering so consistently? Historians point to several features. Concentrated power without checks, free press or elections meant mistakes could not be corrected and abuses could not be exposed. The theory taught that history's direction was certain and that whole classes were enemies, which made mass violence feel justified. And central planning without prices produced shortages that leaders blamed on saboteurs, deepening the terror.

The book records what drew supporters too: rapid industrialization, mass literacy, and formal equality for women, some of which were real achievements. That is part of why the record matters: understanding why decent people supported these regimes helps others recognize the warning signs.

**Key figure.** Vladimir Lenin (1870–1924).

**Key texts.** Lenin's *What Is to Be Done?* (1902) and *The State and Revolution* (1917).

**Origins and context.** Lenin led the Bolshevik Revolution in 1917. Under Stalin, forced collectivization caused famine, including the Holodomor in Ukraine (1932–33). Mao's Great Leap Forward (1958–62) caused a famine with tens of millions of deaths, and the Khmer Rouge (1975–79) killed roughly a quarter of Cambodia's population.

**Other voices (chroniclers).** Robert Conquest, *The Great Terror* (1968); Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, *The Gulag Archipelago* (1973); Frank Dikötter, *Mao's Great Famine* (2010); George Orwell, *Animal Farm* (1945).

**What drew supporters.** Promises of rapid industrialization, mass literacy, and legal equality for women, some of which were delivered.

**Cons.**
- Mass death, repression, and loss of freedom.
- The predicted "withering away of the state" never occurred, which is why it receives a truth score.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** How did communism go from Marx's ideals to famine and terror?
>
> **Historian:** Lenin believed a small party had to seize power and rule for the workers. Once it had all the power, nothing could check it.
>
> **Ari:** Didn't anyone object?
>
> **Historian:** Many did, and were jailed or killed. When there's no free press or elections, mistakes can't be corrected, so a bad harvest policy becomes a famine.
>
> **Ari:** Why did people support it?
>
> **Historian:** Real hopes: land, literacy, factories, equality. Some of that was delivered. That's what makes it a warning, not just a horror story.
>
> **Ari:** What's the warning?
>
> **Historian:** Beware anyone who says the goal is so important that ordinary rules, rights and dissent can be suspended to reach it.

## 54. Fascism

*Group: Political ideology · Told: 25 · Believed: 10 · Likely true: 2%*

**Root assumption.** The nation or race is supreme; outsiders are threats; a strong leader embodies the people's will.

**In depth.**

Fascism is notoriously hard to define, because it was less a theory than a style of politics that changed with circumstances. Historians have nonetheless identified common features. It glorifies the nation, or in Nazism the race, as the supreme value. It promises rebirth after a period of humiliation or decline. It worships a single leader who embodies the people's will, rejects liberal democracy and individual rights, celebrates violence and war as purifying, and names internal and external enemies who are blamed for the nation's troubles. Robert Paxton describes it as a mass movement obsessed with community decline and humiliation, which, in uneasy alliance with traditional elites, abandons democratic liberties and pursues redemptive violence without ethical or legal limits.

Mussolini came to power in 1922 after his Blackshirts' March on Rome, using street violence and the fears of elites who saw him as a bulwark against socialism. Hitler was appointed chancellor in 1933 by conservatives who thought they could control him; within months he had suspended civil liberties and abolished other parties. Nazi Germany went on to wage a war that killed tens of millions and to carry out the Holocaust, the systematic murder of six million Jews alongside Roma, disabled people and others.

Scholars have studied how ordinary people came to participate. Hannah Arendt, observing the trial of Adolf Eichmann, described the "banality of evil": many perpetrators were not monsters but careerists and conformists who stopped thinking about what they were doing. Umberto Eco's essay "Ur-Fascism" lists warning signs, among them the cult of tradition, disagreement treated as treason, fear of difference, appeals to a frustrated middle class, obsession with plots, and contempt for the weak.

The book's near-bottom score reflects war and genocide. Its truth score is very low because fascism's racial claims are refuted by genetics: human genetic variation doesn't divide into the superior and inferior races its ideology required.

**Key figure.** Benito Mussolini (1883–1945).

**Key text.** "The Doctrine of Fascism" (1932), co-written with Giovanni Gentile.

**Origins and context.** Fascism rose after World War I amid humiliation, economic chaos (German hyperinflation in 1923, the Great Depression), and fear of communism. Mussolini took power in 1922; Hitler in 1933. Nazi Germany murdered about six million Jews in the Holocaust and launched World War II.

**Other voices (scholars).** Hannah Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951); Umberto Eco, "Ur-Fascism" (1995); Robert Paxton, *The Anatomy of Fascism* (2004).

**What drew supporters.** Promises of order, national renewal, and jobs after crisis.

**Cons.**
- War and genocide.
- Its racial claims are refuted by genetics, which is why it receives a truth score.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** How could ordinary people support fascism?
>
> **Historian:** It arrived after humiliation, hyperinflation and depression. It promised order, pride and jobs, and it named enemies to blame.
>
> **Ari:** But the violence was obvious.
>
> **Historian:** Often it was dismissed at first as rough talk. Elites thought they could use Hitler and then control him. They were wrong within months.
>
> **Ari:** Were the perpetrators monsters?
>
> **Historian:** Arendt's unsettling finding was that many were ordinary: careerists who followed orders and stopped thinking.
>
> **Ari:** How do you recognize it early?
>
> **Historian:** Watch for leaders who treat disagreement as treason, blame a scapegoated group, glorify violence and attack courts and the press. Each step seems small at the time.

**Try it.**

- Read Umberto Eco's "Ur-Fascism" and see which warning signs appear in the news you follow, from any side.

## 55. Murder for Revenge

*Group: Action · Told: 20 · Believed: 20 · Likely true: not assessed (an action)*

**In summary.** Killing to retaliate for a wrong.

**In depth.**

Revenge is one of the most universal human impulses. Michael McCullough argues it evolved as a deterrent: in ancestral environments without police or courts, a person or group known to retaliate was less likely to be attacked again. Forgiveness evolved alongside it, he argues, as a way to restore valuable relationships once the threat has passed. Both are part of human nature; which one dominates depends heavily on circumstances.

Martin Daly and Margo Wilson's research on homicide found that a large share of killings arise from conflicts over status and respect, insults that escalate, especially between young men. Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen showed that "cultures of honour," which often developed where herding economies made property easy to steal and the state weak, have higher rates of retaliatory violence that persist long after the original conditions vanish. Blood feuds like those governed by Albania's Kanun code have trapped families for generations, with men confined to their homes for years to avoid being targeted.

Revenge also tends to fail on its own terms. Studies of people given a chance to punish someone who wronged them found that they often expected it to feel satisfying but kept ruminating on the offence afterwards, while those who couldn't retaliate moved on sooner. And retaliation rarely ends things: the target's family or group usually sees the revenge as a new injury to be avenged.

Steven Pinker, drawing on the sociologist Norbert Elias, argues that one of history's great reductions in violence came as states took over justice, replacing private vengeance with courts. Where institutions are trusted, people have less need to take revenge themselves. That is the hopeful lesson of this chapter: the urge for revenge is human, but its expression depends on whether there are better ways to get justice.

**Key figures (researchers).** Martin Daly and Margo Wilson; Michael McCullough.

**Key texts.** Daly and Wilson's *Homicide* (1988); McCullough's *Beyond Revenge* (2008); Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen's *Culture of Honor* (1996).

**Origins and context.** McCullough argues revenge evolved as deterrence, and forgiveness evolved alongside it. Blood feuds, like Albania's codified Kanun or America's Hatfield–McCoy feud, show how revenge escalates.

**Other voices.** Norbert Elias; Steven Pinker, *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011), on how state justice reduced violence.

**Pros (as adherents saw it).** Some deterrence of attacks where no justice system exists.

**Cons.** Ends a life, starts cycles of retaliation, and harms both families.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** If someone killed a person I loved, wouldn't revenge be justified?
>
> **Researcher:** The urge would be completely human. It evolved to protect people when there was no one else to protect them.
>
> **Ari:** So why is it wrong?
>
> **Researcher:** It takes another life, and it almost never ends there. Feuds have trapped families for generations, each side avenging the last.
>
> **Ari:** Wouldn't it at least feel better?
>
> **Researcher:** People expect it to. Studies suggest the anger often lingers longer after revenge, not shorter.
>
> **Ari:** Then what helps?
>
> **Researcher:** Justice through courts, support from others, and time. Where people trust that wrongs will be dealt with fairly, they're far less likely to take revenge into their own hands.

**Try it.**

- When anger at someone flares, wait a full day before acting, and talk it through with someone you trust.

## 56. Murder for Territory

*Group: Action · Told: 15 · Believed: 15 · Likely true: not assessed (an action)*

**In summary.** Killing to gain or hold land and resources.

**In depth.**

Fighting over land and resources is ancient. Jane Goodall's team at Gombe watched one chimpanzee community split in two in the 1970s, after which males from the larger group systematically attacked and killed the males of the smaller one over four years and took over its territory. Archaeologist Lawrence Keeley, in *War Before Civilization*, argued from skeletal and site evidence that many prehistoric and tribal societies had high death rates from raiding and warfare, challenging the image of a peaceful primitive past. Thomas Hobbes reasoned that without a government to keep the peace, competition for scarce resources would make life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."

Others push back on any idea that war is inevitable. Douglas Fry points to many societies that settle disputes without warfare and argues that most of human prehistory, among small nomadic bands, saw relatively little organized killing; war became common with settled agriculture, stored wealth and larger groups. The capacity for territorial violence is real, but so is the capacity for peace, and which emerges depends on conditions and institutions.

Humanity has slowly built norms against conquest. Just war theory, from Augustine and Aquinas onward, held that war needs a just cause, legitimate authority and right intention, which territorial greed fails. After two world wars, the UN Charter in 1945 prohibited the use of force against the territorial integrity of other states. Political scientists have documented a marked decline since then in wars that permanently change borders by conquest, though invasions still happen and the norm is constantly tested.

The book places killing for territory near the bottom because it destroys lives, displaces whole populations and seeds future conflict, as conquered peoples rarely forget. The resources it seeks can almost always be obtained through trade, cooperation or negotiation at far lower cost.

**Key figures (thinkers).** Thomas Hobbes; Robert Ardrey.

**Key texts.** Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651); Ardrey's *The Territorial Imperative* (1966); Lawrence Keeley's *War Before Civilization* (1996).

**Origins and context.** Jane Goodall documented a "war" between chimpanzee communities at Gombe (1974–78). Just war theory (Augustine, Aquinas) and the UN Charter (1945) prohibit wars of conquest.

**Other voices.** Douglas Fry, *Beyond War* (2007), arguing war is not inevitable.

**Pros (as adherents saw it).** Resource security for a group.

**Cons.** Death, displacement, and wider conflict.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Haven't humans always fought over land?
>
> **Anthropologist:** Often, yes. Even chimpanzees have been seen waging territorial wars.
>
> **Ari:** So war is just human nature?
>
> **Anthropologist:** The capacity is. But many societies live for generations without war. It depends on conditions: scarcity, stored wealth, and whether there are other ways to settle disputes.
>
> **Ari:** Has anything changed?
>
> **Anthropologist:** After 1945, nations agreed that conquest is illegal. Wars that permanently grab territory became much rarer, though the rule is still tested.
>
> **Ari:** Why does it matter that it's rarer?
>
> **Anthropologist:** Because it shows the norm can work. Trade and treaties usually get people what they need at a fraction of the cost in lives.

**Try it.**

- Learn the story of one border dispute settled peacefully, such as through international arbitration, and what made it possible.

## 57. Rape for Procreation

*Group: Action · Told: 5 · Believed: 5 · Likely true: not assessed (an action)*

**In summary.** Forced sex framed as reproduction, an act found in some animal species and almost certainly somewhere in every lineage's ancestry.

**In depth.**

This chapter is at the very bottom of the scale because it describes one of the gravest harms one person can do to another. It is included because the book scores *everything*, and because it tests the book's own framework.

Randy Thornhill and Craig Palmer argued that rape in humans may be an evolutionary adaptation, or a by-product of other evolved traits, since forced copulation occurs in some species, such as scorpionflies. Their book provoked strong criticism from scientists as well as advocates. Critics pointed out that a large share of victims are children, older people or men, and that many assaults involve no possibility of conception, which a reproductive explanation cannot account for. Research on perpetrators points much more strongly to power, hostility, entitlement and opportunity. Thornhill and Palmer themselves insisted that explaining a behaviour's origin does not excuse it, and that understanding causes could help prevention.

This is where the book's philosophy is tested. Even if an act appears somewhere in the causal chain that led to people existing today, that does not make the act good, any more than a war's role in history makes the war good. Goodness here is measured by what an act does to beings who can feel and understand: violating someone's body and consent causes profound suffering, and survivors often carry trauma for years.

What reduces this harm is concrete: teaching consent from an early age, bystander intervention programmes that train people to notice and interrupt risky situations, believing and supporting survivors, and holding perpetrators accountable. If you or someone you know has been affected, specialist support services exist in most countries, such as RAINN in the United States (rainn.org) and Rape Crisis in the UK, and local emergency services can help anywhere.

**Key figures.** Randy Thornhill and Craig Palmer, who proposed an evolutionary explanation.

**Key texts.** Thornhill and Palmer's *A Natural History of Rape* (2000); *Evolution, Gender, and Rape* (2003), edited by Cheryl Brown Travis, a collection of scientific critiques.

**Origins and context.** Thornhill studied forced copulation in scorpionflies and argued rape in humans might be an adaptation or a byproduct of evolved traits. The book was heavily criticized: victims include children, the elderly, and men, and many assaults cannot lead to pregnancy. Susan Brownmiller's *Against Our Will* (1975) argued rape is primarily about power and intimidation.

**Why it scores so low.** Being part of the causal chain that led to good outcomes, such as any of us existing, doesn't make the act good. Humans can consent and understand what is happening, and survivors often carry lasting trauma. In the author's framework, it sits just above zero.

**In conversation.**

> **Ari:** Why would a book about goodness even include this?
>
> **Counsellor:** Because it scores everything, and because it tests the framework. If anything sits near zero, this does.
>
> **Ari:** Some researchers called it natural, though.
>
> **Counsellor:** That theory was heavily criticized, and the authors themselves said it excuses nothing. "Natural" doesn't mean good. Disease is natural too.
>
> **Ari:** What actually prevents it?
>
> **Counsellor:** Teaching consent, people stepping in when something looks wrong, believing survivors, and real accountability.
>
> **Ari:** And if someone has been through it?
>
> **Counsellor:** Listen, believe them, and let them choose what happens next. Specialist support services can help, at any time, however long ago it was.

**Try it.**

- Learn the basics of bystander intervention: noticing, interrupting safely, and checking in with the person at risk.
- If this chapter brought up something difficult, reach out to a support service or someone you trust.
